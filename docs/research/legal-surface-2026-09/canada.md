## 0. Framework note — read this before the rest

Two regimes stack on Whim simultaneously:

- **PIPEDA** (federal, *Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act*, S.C. 2000, c. 5, Part 1) — applies to the commercial activities of every business in Canada except where a province has "substantially similar" legislation for intra-provincial activity. **Confidence: high.**
- **Quebec's Law 25** — the *Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector*, CQLR c. P-39.1 ("Private Sector Act"), as amended by *An Act to modernize legislative provisions as regards the protection of personal information*, SQ 2021, c. 25 ("Law 25" / former Bill 64) — applies independently to any business established in Quebec or collecting personal information in Quebec, regardless of PIPEDA. Quebec has **not** been declared substantially similar for private-sector purposes since Law 25's 2023 amendments, so the safe assumption is **both regimes apply concurrently** to any Quebec user, and PIPEDA alone applies to users elsewhere in Canada. **Confidence: medium** (declaration status is a judgment call I could not find a definitive 2026 OPC statement on either way — treat Quebec users as double-covered). ⚠

**Major fact that changes the "won't need to change as the app grows" calculus**: on **June 15, 2026** the federal government tabled **Bill C-36**, the *Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act* (PPCDA), which would repeal Part 1 of PIPEDA and replace it with a more prescriptive regime (new regulator — the Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission — direct AMP power, and codified rather than principles-based obligations). It is the third attempt after Bill C-11 (2020) and Bill C-27 (2022), both of which died on the order paper; C-27 died at prorogation in January 2025. C-36 is at first reading only, not law, and its own past two predecessors show federal privacy reform in Canada has a poor track record of actually passing. **Confidence: high on the facts of introduction/history, low on whether/when it becomes law.** Sources: [DLA Piper, "Canada tables Bill C-36"](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/canada-tables-bill-c36-the-protecting-privacy-and-consumer-data-act), [Osler, PPCDA overview](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/reports/the-protecting-privacy-and-consumer-data-act-bill-c-36-key-obligations-and-enforcement-overview/), [Fasken, prorogation kills C-27](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2025/01/prorogations-digital-impact). **Practical takeaway: draft to PIPEDA's principles-based text now; don't over-fit to PIPEDA's current mechanics, because C-36 (if it passes) would require another consent refresh regardless of what you do today.**

## (a) What must the privacy disclosure say — and in what exact form?

### PIPEDA — no prescribed form, but prescribed *content* via two routes

1. **Schedule 1, Principle 4.8 (Openness)** — statute text (paraphrased by secondary sources; I could not get the literal clause text served, see gap note below) requires an organization to make "readily available to individuals specific information about its policies and practices relating to the management of personal information," including what PI it holds, how it's used, a copy of any relevant policy, and how to make an access request. **Confidence: medium** (I know Principle 4.8 exists and its substance from OPC secondary guidance, but was blocked from serving the literal Schedule 1 text directly — the Justice Laws site paginates Schedule 1 separately from the sections and my fetch tool couldn't render it; a human should pull the literal clause). ⚠
2. **Section 6.1 (validity of consent)** — quoted directly, this is the operative test for what the disclosure must communicate, not a form requirement: *"the consent of an individual is only valid if it is reasonable to expect that an individual to whom the organization's activities are directed would understand the nature, purpose and consequences of the collection, use or disclosure of the personal information to which they are consenting."* Source: [PIPEDA s.6.1, laws-lois.justice.gc.ca](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/p-8.6/page-1.html). **Confidence: high** (statute text).
3. **OPC's Guidelines for Obtaining Meaningful Consent (2018, current OPC application guidance)** operationalizes this into four things that must be given "additional emphasis" — not buried in a wall of text: *"What personal information is being collected; With which parties personal information is being shared; For what purposes personal information is collected, used or disclosed; Risk of harm and other consequences."* Source: [OPC, Guidelines for obtaining meaningful consent](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/business-privacy/collecting-personal-information/consent/gl_omc_201805/). **Confidence: high.** No prescribed wording — the guidance is explicitly technology/format-neutral and invites "innovative and creative" presentation (layered notices, just-in-time notices, etc.), so Whim's plain-language approach is compliant *if* those four elements are genuinely emphasized, not just present somewhere in the text.
4. **Definition of "personal information" (s.2(1))**, quoted: *"personal information means information about an identifiable individual."* This is the trigger for whether any of the above applies to a given data element at all — see §(b) for the device-ID and diagnostics analysis. Source: [PIPEDA s.2(1)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/p-8.6/page-1.html). **Confidence: high.**

### Quebec Law 25 — content is prescribed by statute and closer to an exact checklist

Section 8 (as amended) requires that **at or before the time of collection**, a person collecting PI must inform the individual of: the purposes of the collection; the means used to collect it; the rights of access and rectification; the right to withdraw consent; and, where applicable, the name of any third party or category of third party for whom the information is collected, whether the collection is necessary for a service/contract and the consequences of refusal, and the possibility the information will be communicated outside Quebec. (Paraphrase of s.8 confirmed across multiple secondary summaries; I could not get legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca or CanLII to serve the literal French/English text — both blocked WebFetch with HTTP 403. ⚠ **Confidence: medium** on the exact enumeration, **high** that some enumerated-disclosure duty at collection exists.)

**Section 8.1** (this one I got closer to verbatim via secondary quotation): a business that collects PI "using technology that includes functions allowing the person concerned to be identified, located or profiled" must first inform the person of the use of that technology and of the means available to activate the identification/location/profiling functions, and — per secondary commentary — those functions must be **off by default**. This is the Quebec cookie/analytics-opt-in provision and would sweep in any client-side telemetry SDK Whim adds later that fingerprints or profiles a device. Source (secondary, quoting the operative language): [McCarthy Tétrault, "Quebec's Law 25 and Cookies"](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/quebecs-law-25-and-cookies-not-so-cookie-cutter); statute at [P-39.1, legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca](https://legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/p-39.1) (blocked on fetch — a human should verify the literal French/English text). **Confidence: medium.**

**Section 8.2 — this is the closest thing to a "form" requirement in either regime**: a business collecting PI through technological means "must... publish, in clear and simple language, a privacy policy concerning the personal information it collects... and this policy must be published on its website." Secondary summaries are consistent on the "clear and simple terms" language and on a **separate notice duty when the policy is amended** (this is the closest primary-law hook to the "we have to tell users when the deal changes" worry). Source: paraphrase from [Termly, "What is Quebec's Law 25?"](https://termly.io/resources/articles/quebec-law-25/) and consistent across other secondary trackers; I was blocked from the literal legisquebec text. **Confidence: medium** on exact wording, **high** on the existence of the obligation and the "amendment → notice" trigger.

**Section 3.1** requires publishing the **title and contact information of the person in charge of protection of personal information** (by default, "the person exercising the highest authority" — i.e., the owner, unless delegated in writing). This directly answers part of **(f)** below. **Confidence: medium** (consistent secondary paraphrase, statute text not independently verified). ⚠

**No regime prescribes exact boilerplate wording.** Both are principles/outcome-based: PIPEDA cares whether an average person *would understand*; Quebec cares whether the language is *"clear and simple."* Neither requires a specific legal-form document, a specific font size, or magic words — that puts the ball entirely in "is this actually readable and complete," which favors Whim's plain-language draft over legalese, provided the content checklist above is met.

## (b) Consent basis for each specific practice

Baseline test under PIPEDA: **s.5(3)**, quoted — *"An organization may collect, use or disclose personal information only for purposes that a reasonable person would consider are appropriate in the circumstances."* This is a hard outer limit that consent cannot waive (OPC, meaningful-consent guidance, quoted above: *"an individual's consent is not a free pass"*). Under Quebec, the operative default (s.14, paraphrased consistently across sources, not independently verified against primary text ⚠) is that consent must be **"manifest, free and informed, and given for specific purposes"** and, per secondary commentary on the section, must generally be requested **separately for each purpose** that isn't necessary for the contract itself.

### Is an anonymous device ID "personal information" in Canada? (the load-bearing sub-question)

**Short answer: probably yes, under current OPC practice, even though it carries no name.** Sources, with the key move being the "capacity to link" test rather than a name-on-file test:

- PIPEDA's identifiability test from *Gordon v. Canada (Health)*, 2008 FC 258 (quoted via CanLII secondary treatment, primary judgment at [canlii.org](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/fct/doc/2008/2008fc258/2008fc258.html)): information is "about an identifiable individual" where there is **"a serious possibility that an individual could be identified through the use of that information, alone or in combination with other available information."** **Confidence: high** — this is the settled Federal Court test and it is device-agnostic; it does not require a name, only a serious possibility of linkage.
- The OPC has repeatedly found persistent device/browser identifiers to be PI where the collecting organization (or a party it works with) has the **capacity to link the identifier back to a person's behavior over time**, even without a name: see [PIPEDA Report of Findings #2013-017 (Apple, unique device identifiers for targeted advertising)](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2013/pipeda-2013-017/) and reporting on the Bell "Relevant Advertising Program" finding treating device/browser IDs as PI. **Confidence: medium** (I have the Apple finding directly; the Bell citation is via secondary summary only).
- Countervailing point: a device ID used **only** as an opaque counter (never joined to prompts, reports, or any other collected field, rotated/discarded on a schedule, no cross-app tracking) narrows the "combination with other information" risk close to zero, which is the OPC's own escape valve — see the anonymization standard above. **The safer drafting move is not to claim the device ID is "not personal information," but to say plainly what it is used for (rate limits + cost accounting) and that it is never combined with app content or used to build a profile of you** — that's honest, satisfies the disclosure duty either way, and doesn't hinge on winning a contestable identifiability argument. **Confidence: medium**, this is my synthesis/recommendation, not a quoted holding.

## (c) Does anything regulate crash reporting or SDKs *as such*?

**No — in both PIPEDA and Quebec's law, the regulatory hook is always the personal information involved, never the technology or vendor by name.** Neither statute has an "SDK," "crash reporter," or "third-party library" category. Consequences:

- Whim's privacy text should **never name a specific crash-reporting/error-diagnostics vendor** as a promise, because swapping vendors later (a routine engineering decision) would then look like "what Whim sends has changed" even though the actual data practice didn't change. Describe the **practice** ("allowlisted crash and error diagnostics, not linked to your identity") not the **implementation** ("we use [Vendor] SDK version X").
- The one exception where implementation detail *does* become legally load-bearing is **Quebec s.8.1's technology-disclosure duty**, which is triggered by function (*"technology that includes functions allowing the person concerned to be identified, located or profiled"*), not by naming a vendor — so if a future SDK does fingerprinting/profiling, the trigger is the *behavior* of that SDK, not its brand name, and the fix is still to describe the behavior generically in the policy rather than list vendors.
- **Confidence: high** that no Canadian privacy statute regulates SDKs/crash-reporting as a named category; **medium** on the completeness of my search (I did not find a Canada-specific "mobile SDK" regulation, but cannot rule out a narrow sectoral rule I didn't search for). ⚠

## (d) When the policy changes: notice, or fresh consent?

This is a "depends on what changed" answer in both regimes — neither has a bright-line rule that *every* wording change requires re-consent, but both treat some categories of change as requiring a fresh consent event, not just a notice banner.

- **PIPEDA — Principle 4.3 (Consent)** operates purpose-by-purpose: consent obtained for purpose A does not cover a **new purpose** B. OPC's meaningful-consent guidance repeatedly frames consent as tied to specific, named purposes — adding a new purpose (e.g., "we will now also use your prompts to train our own model," a new *recipient* category, or a new *category* of data collected) is functionally a new collection/use event requiring its own consent, not an amendment notice. A **rewording that doesn't change what's collected, why, or who sees it** — including switching the underlying AI vendor for the *same* stated purpose ("your prompt is sent to a third-party AI provider to generate your app"), or adding a *narrower*, safety-motivated practice (crash diagnostics, abuse prevention) that a reasonable person would already expect — is defensible as notice-only. **Confidence: medium** — this is my synthesis of the purpose-specificity principle, not a quoted OPC rule that draws this exact line; a lawyer should confirm before relying on "notice-only" for a vendor swap. ⚠
- **Quebec — s.8.2's amendment-notice duty** (see §(a)) is the one *explicit* statutory hook: Quebec secondary sources are consistent that amending the privacy policy triggers a **notice obligation**, distinct from and lesser than a fresh-consent obligation. Whether a given amendment additionally needs fresh consent still turns on the s.14 purpose-specificity rule (new purpose = new consent), same logic as PIPEDA. **Confidence: medium** (I could not independently verify the s.8.2 amendment-notice text against the primary statute — flagged above). ⚠
- **Practical drafting implication (matches the owner's own instinct)**: write purposes at the *outcome* level ("send what you type to third-party AI services to generate the app," "keep operational logs to run the service securely and prevent abuse," "collect allowlisted, non-identifying crash diagnostics to fix bugs") rather than the *implementation* level ("send to OpenRouter," "use Vendor X's crash SDK"). Changing implementation without changing the outcome-level purpose is the version of "change" most defensibly handled by notice alone under both regimes. Changing the **purpose itself** (new recipient category doing something users weren't told about, a new category of data, monetizing data in a new way) is the version that both regimes point toward needing fresh, specific consent.

## (e) Do the privacy promises become legally enforceable — does over-promising create liability?

**Yes, unambiguously, under the Competition Act — and there is a direct, on-point Canadian precedent, not just a theoretical risk.**

- **Section 52(1)**, quoted: *"No person shall, for the purpose of promoting, directly or indirectly, the supply or use of a product or for the purpose of promoting, directly or indirectly, any business interest, by any means whatever, knowingly or recklessly make a representation to the public that is false or misleading in a material respect."* This is the **criminal** track (fines at the court's discretion or up to 14 years imprisonment on indictment). Source: [Competition Act s.52, laws-lois.justice.gc.ca](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-34/section-52.html). **Confidence: high.**
- **Section 52(4)** (general impression test), quoted: *"the general impression conveyed by a representation as well as its literal meaning shall be taken into account in determining whether or not the representation is false or misleading in a material respect."* This matters directly for privacy copy — it means a technically-true-but-misleading-in-effect sentence ("we never sell your data" while quietly sharing it with an ad partner for something a reasonable reader would call "sharing," not "selling") is caught, not just outright lies. **Confidence: high** (statute text).
- **Section 74.01(1)** is the parallel **civil/administrative** track (reviewable conduct before the Competition Tribunal — no need to prove criminal intent), covering the same "false or misleading in a material respect" representation, with orders that can include a corrective-notice publication requirement and administrative monetary penalties. **Confidence: high** on the existence and general shape; I did not get the exact AMP dollar cap for a first-time individual/small-business respondent in this pass — a lawyer should pull current s.74.1 figures before finalizing anything the policy promises "for good." ⚠
- **The direct precedent — Facebook, 2020**: the Competition Bureau investigated Facebook's privacy representations (2012–2018 conduct) and Facebook settled with the Competition Tribunal for a **$9 million administrative monetary penalty plus $500,000 in investigation costs**, on the theory that Facebook's public claims about users' ability to control who saw their personal information were false or misleading given how broadly the data was actually shared with third-party app developers. This was reported as **the first Canadian case treating a privacy policy's own representations as subject to misleading-advertising law**, not merely a privacy-regulator matter. Source: [Competition Bureau press release, canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2020/05/facebook-to-pay-9-million-penalty-to-settle-competition-bureau-concerns-about-misleading-privacy-claims.html); analysis at [Stikeman Elliott](https://stikeman.com/en-CA/kh/competitor/facebook-pays-multimillion-dollar-penalty-in-first-case-in-canada) and [nNovation](https://nnovation.com/facebooks-9m-settlement-with-canadas-competition-bureau-makes-history/). **Confidence: high** (multiple corroborating sources on the settlement amount and date, May 19, 2020).
- **Private right of action**: **s.36(1)**, paraphrased consistently across sources (statute text at [laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-34/section-36.html](https://laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-34/section-36.html), not independently quoted verbatim this pass ⚠), lets **any person who suffered loss or damage** as a result of conduct contrary to Part VI (which includes s.52) sue for actual damages plus investigation/litigation costs — this is on top of Bureau enforcement, and on top of any Quebec s.93.1 punitive-damages claim (see §(f)). Punitive damages are **not** available under s.36 itself (a limitation confirmed by case law — *Wong v. Sony of Canada Ltd.*). **Confidence: medium-high.**

**Bottom line for the drafting principle already adopted ("promise everything Whim means to do, with room to spare, and nothing past that")**: this is not just good practice, it is the legally correct posture. Every sentence in the privacy text is a "representation to the public" a regulator or a plaintiff can hold Whim to at its literal *and* general-impression meaning. Absolute words — "never," "nobody," "no one ever" — are the highest-risk phrasing precisely because they're the easiest to falsify later and the hardest to walk back with a "notice only" amendment (see §(d)): if the promise is later false even in one edge case (e.g., a lawful court order compels disclosure, which no privacy law can promise away), that is a *material* false representation, not a technicality.

## (f) Other obligations, and any size-based exemptions

- **No size threshold exempts Whim from either PIPEDA or Law 25.** PIPEDA applies to any organization engaged in "commercial activity" regardless of size or revenue — there is no small-business carve-out for the substantive obligations (there are narrower distinctions for federal works/undertakings vs. provincially-regulated business, irrelevant here). Law 25 likewise applies to "every person carrying on an enterprise" without a size floor, though its **administrative monetary penalty regime is calibrated to be proportionate** — commentary notes AMPs "up to CAD $10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover" for the general tier and up to "$25 million or 4%" for the more serious tier, with **individual respondents capped lower (around $50,000 / $100,000)** — but a solo-founder company is still a "person carrying on an enterprise," not automatically an "individual," so don't assume the lower individual cap applies to AnyCognition-the-business. **Confidence: medium** on the exact caps (multiple consistent secondary sources, primary CQLR sections 90.1–90.12 not independently fetched — blocked by 403 both direct and via CanLII). ⚠
- **A named privacy contact is mandatory in Quebec** (s.3.1, see §(a)) — publish a name/title and contact channel for "the person in charge of the protection of personal information," which by default is the person with the highest authority (i.e., the owner) unless delegated in writing. PIPEDA's Openness principle (4.8) similarly expects a means of contacting someone accountable for privacy practices, though it is less prescriptive about title/format. **Action item, not just a research note: this needs to exist in the app/policy regardless of what else changes.** **Confidence: medium** (Quebec text not independently verified; PIPEDA openness principle well-established).
- **Cross-border transfer impact assessment is mandatory before the first prompt ever leaves Quebec for OpenRouter/model vendors** — see §17 quoted in §(b): *"Before communicating personal information outside Québec, a person carrying on an enterprise must conduct a privacy impact assessment,"* considering sensitivity, purpose, contractual/other protective measures, and the destination's legal framework, with communication permitted only if the assessment shows the information "would receive adequate protection," and requiring **a written agreement with the recipient** addressing the assessment's results and any risk-mitigation terms. Source (secondary, closely quoting): [WatchDog Security, Law 25 §17](https://watchdogsecurity.io/law25/cross-border-transfers); statute at [P-39.1 s.17](https://legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/p-39.1) (blocked on fetch ⚠). **Confidence: medium-high** on substance, this is one of the most consistently and precisely reported provisions of Law 25 across independent secondary sources even though I couldn't fetch primary text directly. **This is a concrete pre-launch action item, not just disclosure text — the assessment and the OpenRouter/vendor agreement need to actually exist**, separately from whatever the privacy policy says.
- **No general Canadian equivalent of a GDPR-style "EU representative" requirement** — I found no Canadian statute requiring a foreign or non-resident business to appoint a local representative or agent for service, unlike the EU/UK GDPR Article 27 mechanism. This appears to be a genuine asymmetry in Whim's favor (no representative needed to sell into Canada from Canada, obviously, but also nothing extra required because it's a solo operation). **Confidence: medium** (absence-of-evidence result — I did not find a requirement, but did not exhaustively search every provincial private-sector statute; Alberta/BC PIPA equivalents were out of scope per the task's jurisdiction list). ⚠
- **Quebec's privacy-impact-assessment duty is broader than just cross-border transfers** — secondary sources describe a companion obligation (commonly cited as s.3.3) to conduct a PIA before *"any project of acquisition, development or overhaul of an information system or electronic service delivery involving the collection, use, communication, keeping or destruction of personal information."** If true as commonly summarized, a from-scratch mobile app with a novel AI-generation pipeline plausibly already triggered this at build time, separate from the transfer-specific §17 assessment. **Confidence: low** — I could not independently verify this section's text or number in this pass; flag for a lawyer, this is exactly the kind of "we should have done a PIA already" gap that's cheap to fix now and expensive to discover later. ⚠
- **Automated decision-making disclosure (Quebec s.12.1)**: if Whim ever renders a decision "based exclusively on automated processing" that affects a user (imagine: an automated fraud/abuse determination that silently throttles or bans a device with no human review), Quebec law requires informing the person no later than when they're told of the decision, and — on request — explaining the personal information used and the principal factors, plus offering a chance to have a human review it. Likely **not yet triggered** by rate-limiting alone (a soft limit is not usually "a decision" in the legal sense), but **would be triggered** by an automated permanent ban/report-driven takedown with no human in the loop — worth keeping in mind as the abuse-prevention system matures. **Confidence: medium** (consistent secondary paraphrase, not independently verified against primary text). ⚠
- **Competition Act's "adequate and proper test" substantiation duty** (s.74.01(1)(b)/(b.1), currently scoped to performance/efficacy/environmental claims by its text) is a useful *analogy* even where it doesn't literally apply: the Bureau's Facebook enforcement shows it is willing to treat an unsubstantiated *privacy* claim the same way it treats an unsubstantiated *performance* claim — false-or-misleading-in-material-respect doesn't require the specialized "adequate and proper test" clause to bite, s.52/74.01(1)(a) alone is enough, as Facebook shows. **Confidence: medium** (reasoning by analogy from the general provision plus the one precedent, not a quoted extension of the specific substantiation clause).

## Appendix — other quoted provisions worth keeping handy

**PIPEDA s.7 exceptions** (collection/use/disclosure without knowledge or consent) — relevant paragraphs for Whim's threat model, paraphrased from statute (verified via [laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/FullText.html](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/FullText.html), **confidence: medium**, my fetch tool rendered a structured paraphrase rather than the literal clause text, re-verify before citing in a legal document ⚠): collection is permitted without consent where clearly in the individual's own interest and timely consent is impossible (7(1)(a)); disclosure is permitted without consent for legal/fraud investigations, court orders, or government demand (7(3)). None of these look like they license the *routine* practices at issue here (AI-prompt transfer, device ID, diagnostics) — those all need an affirmative consent-basis analysis under §(b), not a s.7 exception.

**PIPEDA s.10.1** (breach of security safeguards), quoted: *"An organization shall report to the Commissioner any breach of security safeguards involving personal information under its control if it is reasonable in the circumstances to believe that the breach creates a real risk of significant harm to an individual."* And, separately: *"Unless otherwise prohibited by law, an organization shall notify an individual of any breach of security safeguards involving the individual's personal information under the organization's control if it is reasonable in the circumstances to believe that the breach creates a real risk of significant harm to the individual."* Real risk of significant harm includes bodily harm, humiliation, damage to reputation, financial loss, identity theft, negative credit-record effect, and property damage/loss; assessment factors are sensitivity of the information and probability of misuse. Source: [PIPEDA s.10.1](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/p-8.6/section-10.1.html). **Confidence: high** (quoted directly from the statute page). There is also a mandatory **breach record-keeping duty** (every breach, not just reportable ones) under s.10.3, not independently confirmed this pass. ⚠ Quebec's parallel regime (s.3.5–3.8, "confidentiality incident") uses a near-identical **"risk of serious injury"** trigger, assessed by sensitivity of the information, anticipated consequences of its use, and likelihood of injurious use, with notification duties running to both the CAI and affected individuals. **Confidence: medium** (consistent secondary paraphrase across multiple law-firm summaries; primary text not independently fetched). ⚠ **This needs an actual incident-response runbook regardless of what the privacy text says** — both regimes make notification of a *bad* event mandatory on facts, not on what was promised in advance.

**Quebec s.93.1** (punitive damages / private right of action), paraphrased consistently across sources (not independently verified against primary text ⚠): a court "shall award punitive damages of not less than $1,000" where an "unlawful infringement" of a right under the Act (or arts. 35–40 of the Civil Code) is intentional or grossly negligent and causes injury — actual harm and causation must still be shown, but the $1,000 floor removes the usual punitive-damages balancing analysis. **Confidence: medium.** This exists **in addition to** CAI administrative penalties and **in addition to** Competition Act exposure — three independent liability tracks stack on the same bad fact pattern (e.g., a careless data leak that also happens to make an existing privacy promise false).
