## PART 1 — APPLE

### 1.1 Guideline 5.1.1 "Data Collection and Storage" (verbatim, current text)

Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ (fetched 2026-09-23). **High confidence** — primary source, live page.

> **(i) Privacy Policies:** All apps must include a link to their privacy policy in the App Store Connect metadata field and within the app in an easily accessible manner. The privacy policy must clearly and explicitly:
> - Identify what data, if any, the app/service collects, how it collects that data, and all uses of that data.
> - Confirm that any third party with whom an app shares user data ... will provide the same or equal protection of user data as stated in the app's privacy policy and required by these Guidelines.
> - Explain its data retention/deletion policies and describe how a user can revoke consent and/or request deletion of the user's data.
>
> **(ii) Permission:** Apps that collect user or usage data must secure user consent for the collection, even if such data is considered to be anonymous at the time of or immediately following collection. Paid functionality must not be dependent on or require a user to grant access to this data. Apps must also provide the customer with an easily accessible and understandable way to withdraw consent. ... Apps that collect data for a legitimate interest without consent by relying on the terms of the [GDPR] or similar statute must comply with all terms of that law.
>
> **(iii) Data Minimization:** Apps should only request access to data relevant to the core functionality of the app and should only collect and use data that is required to accomplish the relevant task.

Notable for Whim: (ii) explicitly allows a GDPR "legitimate interest" basis without consent, **but only if you fully comply with GDPR's own conditions for that basis** — Apple doesn't independently bless it. This matters for the anonymous device ID (see §3.2).

### 1.2 Guideline 5.1.2 "Data Use and Sharing" — the third-party-AI clause

**High confidence, this is the load-bearing quote for question (b):**

> **(i)** Unless otherwise permitted by law, you may not use, transmit, or share someone's personal data without first obtaining their permission. You must provide access to information about how and where the data will be used. **You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so.** Data collected from apps may only be shared with third parties to improve the app or serve advertising (in compliance with the Apple Developer Program License Agreement). ... Apps that share user data without user consent or otherwise complying with data privacy laws may be removed from sale and may result in your removal from the Apple Developer Program.

The "including with third-party AI" clause was added company-wide 2025-11-13 (per contemporaneous press: TechCrunch, Cult of Mac, dev.to writeups — **medium confidence** on the exact effective date since it's not in Apple's changelog text I fetched, only secondary reporting). It makes explicit consent-before-first-transmission a **platform rule**, independent of whatever legal basis GDPR/PIPEDA would otherwise allow. Secondary-source commentary (medium confidence, not Apple's own text) claims narrow exceptions: on-device inference, first-party-operated AI backends, and public API calls carrying no personal data. None of that carve-out language is in Apple's guideline text itself — treat it as industry interpretation, not policy text. **For Whim: sending the user's spoken/typed prompt to OpenRouter → third-party model vendors to generate app code is squarely "sharing personal data with third-party AI" under the literal guideline text**, regardless of whether the prompt contains anything that looks like PII, because the guideline doesn't gate on data category — it gates on the AI-sharing act itself. Apple will expect a specific, contextual, in-app disclosure naming the practice (a blanket privacy-policy sentence is reported as an actual rejection pattern, medium confidence from developer-forum/blog sources, not an Apple guideline statement).

Also relevant, same section:
> **(ii)** Data collected for one purpose may not be repurposed without further consent unless otherwise explicitly permitted by law.
> **(iii)** Apps should not attempt to surreptitiously build a user profile based on collected data...

### 1.3 Guideline 1.2 "User-Generated Content" (verbatim)

**High confidence**, applies to Whim's mini-app reporting flow and any user prompts/output surfaced to others (currently none are shared between users, per stated facts — flag if that changes):

> Apps with user-generated content or social networking services must include:
> - A method for filtering objectionable material from being posted to the app
> - A mechanism to report offensive content and timely responses to concerns
> - The ability to block abusive users from the service
> - Published contact information so users can easily reach you

Since Whim mini-apps are private per-user (no sharing/posting to other users, no social feed), 1.2's "posted to the app" trigger arguably doesn't apply *unless* Apple treats AI-generated app content as UGC by analogy (their **1.2.1 "Creator Content"** carve-out is for a different fact pattern — creators publishing to a shared marketplace). **Medium confidence**: Whim likely doesn't need the four UGC safeguards today because there is no cross-user content surface, but the existing "report an app" feature already gives Apple a defensible answer if asked, and costs nothing to keep.

### 1.4 App Privacy details ("nutrition label") — operative definitions

Source: Apple's App Privacy Details definitions page (fetched via search digest 2026-09-23, cross-checked against Apple Developer + support.apple.com framing). **High confidence** on the definitions text, since multiple independent summaries agree verbatim on wording.

- **"Collect"**: *"transmitting data off the device in a way that allows you and/or your third-party partners to access it for a period longer than what is necessary to service the transmitted request in real time."* → Data that never leaves the device (mini-app user data, per Whim's design) is **not "collected"** and needs no privacy-label entry. This is the textual basis for Whim's core promise.
- **"Data Linked to You"**: linked to identity via account/device/etc., unless you strip direct identifiers *before* collection and never re-link. **Device identifiers used for rate-limiting/cost-ledger are "linked" data** under this definition — an anonymous device ID is still a "device" link, and Apple's label schema has an explicit "Device ID" data type for exactly this.
- **"Data Not Linked to You"**: requires de-identification *before* collection plus no re-linking after.
- **"Tracking"**: linking data about a user/device with **third-party data** for targeted advertising/ad measurement, or sharing with a data broker. Whim's device ID (used only for first-party rate limiting/cost accounting, never joined to third-party ad data, no ads) is **not "tracking"** under this definition — high confidence this lets Whim answer "No" to the App Tracking Transparency question.
- **Optional-disclosure exemption** (all four must hold): not used for tracking; not used for third-party/developer advertising or "other purposes"; collection is infrequent, outside primary functionality, and optional for the user; and is user-provided via a clear affirmative in-app choice each time. Server operational logs and crash diagnostics **do not qualify** for this exemption (they're not "user-provided each time"); they must be declared as regular data types (see below) rather than omitted.

Practical mapping for Whim's privacy label (medium confidence — this is Whim-specific application, not verbatim Apple text):
| Practice | Likely App Privacy label answer |
|---|---|
| Voice/text prompt sent to server → OpenRouter → model vendor | "Collected" — likely under "User Content" (or "Other User Content"), Linked to You (tied to device ID at minimum), not used for tracking, purpose = App Functionality |
| Anonymous device ID for rate limits/cost ledger | "Collected", type "Device ID", Linked to You, not Tracking, purpose = App Functionality |
| Crash/error diagnostics (planned) | "Collected", type "Crash Data" / "Performance Data" / "Diagnostics", declare Linked or Not-Linked depending on whether it's tied to the device ID |
| Mini-app user data (stays on device) | Not "collected" at all — omit |
| User abuse reports | "Collected", likely "User Content" + "Other Data" (whatever the report contains), Linked to You |

### 1.5 Privacy manifest (`PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy`) and required-reason APIs

**High confidence**, this is a distinct, code-level obligation from the privacy label, enforced by App Store Connect ingestion since **May 1, 2024** for any SDK (including your own code, if it ships as a third-party SDK to itself... practically: any code) using a "required reason" API. Declared categories include (verbatim reason codes from Apple's official reason strings, cross-checked): `NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryFileTimestamp` (reason `C617.1` etc.), `NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategorySystemBootTime` (`35F9.1`), `NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryDiskSpace` (`E174.1`), `NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryUserDefaults` (`CA92.1`), plus others (active keyboard extensions). **This has nothing to do with what data leaves the device** — it's a code-behavior disclosure required even for on-device-only functionality (e.g. Whim's storage engine reading/writing SQLite, timestamps, etc., could trigger the FileTimestamp or UserDefaults reason). `NSPrivacyCollectedDataTypes` is the separate declaration mirroring the App Privacy label, closed-list, includes `NSPrivacyCollectedDataTypeCrashData` and an `NSPrivacyCollectedDataTypeOtherDataTypes` catch-all. **Action item, not just policy text**: whoever builds the iOS binary needs to audit third-party dependencies (React Native, any native modules, op-sqlite, isomorphic-git polyfills) for required-reason API usage — this is a build-time gate, not a legal-writing task, but it is a real submission blocker independent of the privacy policy.

### 1.6 Standard EULA vs custom EULA

**High confidence**, from Apple's own minimum-terms page (`apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/dev/minterms/`) plus the Developer Program License Agreement:

- If you supply no EULA, Apple's **Standard EULA** (`apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/dev/stdeula/`) applies automatically and already satisfies all minimum terms.
- A **custom EULA** may add terms or exceed the minimums, but must include, at minimum: (1) acknowledgment the EULA is between developer and end-user only, not Apple; (2) a license scope limited to Apple-branded products the user owns/controls; (3) developer, not Apple, is responsible for maintenance/support; (4) Apple has no warranty obligation; (5) developer, not Apple, is responsible for product claims; (6) developer, not Apple, is responsible for IP-infringement claims; (7) export/sanctions-compliance representations by the end-user; (8) developer's name/address and contact info for user questions/complaints/claims; (9) end-user compliance with applicable third-party agreement terms (e.g. wireless carrier ToS); (10) Apple and its subsidiaries are third-party beneficiaries entitled to enforce the EULA. A custom EULA **cannot contradict** these minimums or Apple's Usage Rules.
- **Practical read**: unless Whim needs specific terms (arbitration clause, liability caps beyond Apple's defaults, a specific dispute-resolution forum, IP ownership language for AI-generated mini-app code), the Standard EULA is simpler and needs zero maintenance. Custom EULA is only worth the overhead if there's a concrete term Whim needs that the standard doesn't give — e.g., clarifying ownership/license of AI-generated mini-app code, since that's Whim-specific and not covered by Apple's boilerplate.

### 1.7 Editing App Privacy answers without a new build

**Medium-high confidence**: Apple's own guidance and consistent developer-forum reporting says App Privacy answers in App Store Connect **can be updated at any time without submitting a new build**, and take effect once submitted (subject to your own review/typo-checking — there's no App Review gate on privacy-answer edits themselves). One caveat surfaced in forum threads: some *tracking-removal* edits appear to reference "the version currently live on the Store" rather than an in-flight unreviewed build, which can create a lag when trying to *reduce* declared tracking ahead of the code that stops it — not relevant to Whim's direction of travel (adding, not removing, declared practices), so low practical risk, but note it since decision-relevant: it argues for shipping the privacy-label update *at or after* the code that starts a new practice, not before.

## PART 2 — GOOGLE PLAY

### 2.1 User Data policy — required privacy policy contents

Source: Play Console Help "User Data" policy page (fetched 2026-09-23). **High confidence.**

A privacy policy must include, verbatim requirements:
> - Developer information and a privacy point of contact or a mechanism to submit inquiries
> - Disclosure of the types of personal and sensitive user data your app accesses, collects, uses, and shares
> - Secure data handling procedures for personal and sensitive user data
> - The developer's data retention and deletion policy

Format requirement: must be "clearly labeled as a privacy policy," reachable via "an active, publicly accessible and non-geofenced URL (no PDFs)." No prescribed exact wording (unlike some other regimes) — content requirements, not a form-of-words mandate.

On sharing/service providers: transfers "as necessary to service providers or for legal reasons" are contemplated as legitimate, and "selling" data is separately prohibited (defined as exchange for **monetary consideration**, with a carve-out for user-initiated transfers) — so a paid OpenRouter/model-vendor relationship is not "selling" under this definition as long as it isn't consideration paid *to Whim* for the data itself.

On persistent identifiers: don't link hardware-persistent IDs (IMEI/IMSI/SIM serial) to personal data or to resettable IDs, except narrow telephony/EMM cases with prominent disclosure. **Whim's anonymous device ID should be a resettable/app-scoped identifier, not a hardware serial**, to stay clear of this restriction — worth confirming with engineering what ID is actually used.

### 2.2 Prominent disclosure & consent — when it's required, what counts

Source: Play Console Help "Best practices for prominent disclosure and consent" (fetched 2026-09-23). **High confidence** on the quoted language.

> Prominent disclosure [is required] in cases where users may not reasonably expect their personal and sensitive user data to be required for policy-compliant features or functionality.

Requirements for the disclosure itself: must be **in-app** (not just in the store listing or a website), shown during "normal usage" without requiring menu navigation, must state what data is accessed/collected and how it will be used/shared, and must **precede** any consent prompt or runtime permission request. Consent itself needs "a distinct, affirmative user action" — "clear and friendly language" (e.g. "Agree," not "Allow access"), with an equally easy decline option; navigating away is not deemed consent. Explicitly: **the prominent-disclosure requirement is separate from, and not satisfied by, the privacy policy or the Data safety form** — all three obligations are independent and cumulative.

**Applied to Whim**: sending a user's prompt to a third-party AI model to generate their app is not something a typical user would necessarily expect without being told (the product's whole framing is "talk to Whim," not "your words go to five different AI companies") — this plausibly triggers the prominent-disclosure bar even absent an Apple-style explicit rule, because it's data leaving the device to serve a purpose the user might not picture unprompted. **Medium confidence** this specific scenario would be treated as needing prominent disclosure by Google (no enforcement case directly on point found), but it tracks the stated rationale closely enough to treat as the working assumption.

### 2.3 Data safety section — definitions

Source: Play Console Help "Provide information for Google Play's Data safety section" (fetched 2026-09-23). **High confidence** on the definitional quotes.

- **Collection** = *"Transmitting data from your app off a user's device"* — includes transmission by any library/SDK/webview under the app's control. Same on-device/off-device line as Apple's.
- **Sharing** = *"Transferring user data collected from your app to a third party"* — server-to-server, on-device inter-app transfer, or via SDK/webview all count.
- **Service-provider exemption**: an entity that "processes user data on behalf of the developer and based on the developer's instructions" (e.g., a cloud host or analytics vendor processing solely for the developer) is **not** "sharing" and doesn't need the sharing disclosure — **but the underlying collection still must be disclosed**. This is the single most decision-relevant definition for Whim: **whether OpenRouter and the underlying model vendors qualify as "service providers" (collection-only disclosure) vs. independent third-party recipients (sharing disclosure) turns on their contractual instructions and whether they can use the data for their own purposes** (e.g., model training). If any vendor in the chain can use prompts for its own model improvement, that recipient is not acting solely "on the developer's instructions" and the transfer must be declared as **sharing**, not merely collection. **Action item for engineering/ops, not just docs**: get each OpenRouter-routed model vendor's data-use terms confirmed (no training on inputs, or opt-out exercised) before finalizing this declaration either way.
- **Ephemeral-processing exemption**: data "access[ed] and us[ed] while stored only in memory and retained no longer than necessary to service the specific request in real time" needs the collection question answered in the form but **won't display publicly** in the Data safety section. Explicitly cannot be used for building ad/user profiles. **This likely doesn't cover Whim's AI-generation flow** if the vendor retains the prompt at all post-response (even briefly, for abuse monitoring) — treat as not qualifying unless a specific vendor's zero-retention terms are confirmed.
- **Optional vs Required**: "optional" = user can decline and still use the app (or opt out); "required" = necessary for the app's primary functionality. **Sending the prompt to AI is Required** (it's the product), the **device ID for rate limits is arguably Required** (limits apply to everyone), **crash diagnostics should be framed as Required-but-limited-purpose or, if genuinely skippable, Optional** — worth an explicit design decision, since Google's opt-out rule for "optional" data types requires it be honorable **globally**, not region-gated.
- **Crash/diagnostics/device IDs**: no special exemption "for rate-limiting use cases" — they fall under ordinary categories ("App info and performance," "Device or other IDs") and must be declared per actual practice like anything else.

### 2.4 AI-Generated Content policy — in-app reporting

Source: Play Console Help "Understanding Google Play's AI-Generated Content policy" + contemporaneous 2023-10 policy-launch coverage (TechCrunch, MediaNama). **High confidence** on scope and the reporting requirement (multiple independent sources converge on the same requirement, though I could not pull Apple/Google's exact clause text verbatim from the primary page — the fetched primary page's overview section didn't spell out the reporting clause itself, so that specific requirement is sourced from secondary reporting at policy-launch time, cross-checked against Whim's own existing "report an app" feature which was evidently built to satisfy exactly this).

- **Scope**: apps that generate content via AI from text/voice/image prompts (text-to-text chatbots, text/voice/image-to-image, synthetic voice/video of real people). Whim's "generate a mini-app from a prompt" flow is text-to-code, which the policy's stated scope (content generated from prompt input) plausibly reaches even though "code" isn't one of the enumerated content modalities in what I could confirm — **medium confidence** on applicability to code-generation specifically, since all example content types quoted are media (text/image/voice/video), not application code. Given Whim already ships a reporting feature, this is moot in practice regardless of the policy's precise boundary.
- **Requirement** (secondary-source consensus): an **in-app** mechanism for users to report/flag offensive or policy-violating AI output **without leaving the app**, feeding into the developer's own moderation/filtering.
- **Prohibited outputs**: non-consensual deepfake sexual content, scam-facilitating synthetic voice/video, self-harm-encouraging content, election disinformation, bullying/harassment content, sexually-gratifying-app intent, malicious code generation. The malicious-code clause is worth flagging to product/eng even though it's not a privacy-text question — Whim generates arbitrary app code from prompts, so a policy-compliance question exists around what guardrails the generation pipeline has against malicious-code prompts, separate from the privacy-disclosure work this file is about.

### 2.5 Editing Data safety answers without a new build

**Medium-high confidence** — consistent across Play Console help and third-party integration guides: the Data safety form is a **Play Console metadata object, edited and republished independently of the app binary**; changes can be submitted at any time and take effect on their own timeline (Google states changes can take a period of time — commonly cited as "up to a few days" in older FAQ text — to propagate to the live Store listing, not tied to app-review of a build). Functionally symmetric with Apple's App Privacy answers (§1.7): **both stores let you update the disclosure surface without shipping new binary code**, which matters for Whim's stated worry about "every consent change forces a new agreement screen" — that re-consent friction is about your *own in-app* consent UX and privacy-policy versioning, not about store mechanics; the stores themselves don't force a new build to update declared practices.

## PART 3 — CROSS-JURISDICTION LEGAL QUESTIONS (a)–(f)

Applicable regimes, given the facts: **PIPEDA** (federal Canadian law, developer's home jurisdiction — applies to “commercial activity” with no size floor); **Quebec's Law 25** (extraterritorial — applies to any organization handling personal information of a person located in Quebec, "regardless of where the organization itself is located," **no revenue/size threshold**, and Canada-wide reach through Quebec users is close to certain for a public worldwide app); **GDPR** (EU/EEA users, once the app is "offered" to them, which a public store listing selected for those countries plausibly satisfies — see §3.4); **CCPA/CPRA** (only if California-specific thresholds are met — see §3.6, likely **not** applicable to a solo-dev app at this stage); **FTC Act §5** (US federal deception/unfairness law — applies to promises made to US consumers regardless of company size or location, if the FTC has jurisdiction, which it generally does for US-facing digital products).

### (a) What MUST a privacy policy say, and must any of it be in exact wording?

**No jurisdiction here requires a specific form-of-words boilerplate** — all use content-requirement lists, not a fill-in-the-blank template.

- **Apple 5.1.1(i)**: policy must identify data collected/how/uses; confirm equal protection from third-party recipients; explain retention/deletion and how to revoke consent/request deletion. (Quoted §1.1.) **High confidence.**
- **Play User Data policy**: contact info, types of data accessed/collected/used/shared, security practices, retention/deletion policy; hosted at a clearly-labeled, public, non-geofenced URL, no PDF. (Quoted §2.1.) **High confidence.**
- **GDPR Art. 13** (personal data collected from the subject): a closed, exhaustive list — identity/contact details of controller (and DPO if any), purposes and legal basis for each purpose, legitimate interests relied on (if that basis is used), recipients or categories of recipients, intent/details of any international transfer and its safeguard, retention period or the criteria to set it, and the existence of each data-subject right (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, portability, withdraw consent, complain to a regulator). **High confidence** — this is Article 13's own text as summarized by every legal source checked; "the list is closed... every item must appear or the notice is non-compliant" per practitioner commentary (medium confidence on that characterization, though the underlying Art.13 list itself is high confidence).
- **PIPEDA / OPC meaningful-consent guidance**: no fixed form, but guidance names four things that must be made "readily available" and specifically emphasized: what's collected, who it's shared with, why (in sufficient detail to be "meaningful"), and the risks/consequences. **High confidence** on the OPC's own four-part framing.
- **Law 25**: privacy policy content requirements largely track Quebec's Act (modeled on GDPR); distinct hard requirement not shared by the others: **publish the name/title and contact details of the privacy officer** ("person in charge of the protection of personal information") — by default this is whoever holds the "highest authority" in the org (i.e., the owner, absent written delegation) — on the website or by "other appropriate means" if there's no website. **High confidence** on the designation-and-publication rule; this is a Whim-specific must-do that's easy to miss since it isn't an Apple/Play requirement.

**Answer to "exact form"**: no. Every regime above is substance-based. The closest thing to a fixed form is Law 25's mandated identification of a named/titled contact, and GDPR's closed list of *topics* (not wording) under Art. 13.

### (c) Does anything regulate crash reporting or SDKs AS SUCH?

**High confidence: no.** Neither Apple's guidelines, Google's policies, GDPR, PIPEDA, nor Law 25 regulate "crash reporting" or "an SDK" as a category in itself. All of them regulate the **personal data that flows through** whatever mechanism is used — Apple's privacy-manifest regime (§1.5) is the closest thing to an SDK-as-such rule, but even that is framed as a data/API-behavior disclosure requirement triggered by *what the code does* (accessing timestamps, disk space, etc.), not a rule "about" crash-reporting SDKs by name. Practical implication for the owner's "avoid tying promises to implementation" principle: the consent text should describe the **practice** ("we collect crash and error information to keep the app working, not linked to your identity") rather than naming any specific vendor or SDK — this is both legally sufficient everywhere checked and matches the stated goal of not needing to re-consent users when the vendor changes.

### (d) Policy changes: notice only, or fresh consent — and for which kinds of change?

- **FTC (US)**: **High confidence**, two on-point enforcement precedents. *Gateway Learning* (2004) — FTC held that **retroactively applying a materially changed policy to previously-collected data**, and failing to give adequate notice of the change, was both unfair and deceptive; the settlement barred retroactive application of material changes **without consumer consent**. *1Health.io* (2023) — same principle applied to a genetic-data company; FTC's 2024 guidance explicitly warns that "quietly changing" privacy terms — especially adopting more permissive practices via unannounced retroactive amendment — can itself be an unfair/deceptive practice. **Rule of thumb from these cases**: notice-only is fine for narrower/clarifying changes; a **new purpose or new recipient class**, especially applied to data already collected, needs affirmative notice at minimum, and courts/FTC have accepted "fresh consent" as the remedy when it wasn't obtained upfront. Mere rewording that doesn't change practice is not treated as requiring anything beyond publishing the update.
- **GDPR**: **High confidence** on the underlying principle, **medium** on exact triggers. Consent (Art. 6(1)(a)) is purpose-bound (Recital 32); if processing changes to a **new purpose** not covered by the original consent, that consent "may no longer be valid" — implying fresh consent is needed. Where the legal basis is legitimate interest rather than consent, the relevant duty is closer to updated Art. 13 disclosure (a new recipient or purpose must be disclosed) rather than "consent" per se, since there's no consent to refresh. **Net for Whim**: a **new AI-model vendor / new recipient category** or a **genuinely new purpose** (e.g., starting to use prompts for ad targeting, which the owner's principles rule out anyway) would need fresh consent under a consent-based reading, or at minimum prominent updated notice under a legitimate-interest reading; a reworded description of an unchanged practice does not.
- **PIPEDA/Law 25**: no bright-line "notice vs. consent" test found in primary guidance beyond the general "meaningful consent" principle (§ (a)) — logically, the same "new purpose needs new understanding, hence new consent" logic applies, since OPC's four-factor meaningful-consent test is evaluated as of the point of collection/use, and a materially different use wouldn't have been within what the individual could have "reasonably expected" from the original disclosure.
- **Apple/Play (store mechanics)**: as established (§1.7, §2.5), you can update the store-facing disclosure (App Privacy / Data safety) at any time without a new build — that's a **mechanical** capability, separate from whether the underlying law requires fresh *user* consent for the change. Don't conflate "I can edit the Play Console form today" with "I don't need to re-prompt my users."

**Practical synthesis for the "promise everything, nothing past that" principle**: because the exposure above is specifically for *new purposes* and *new recipients* — never for wording — the engineering-side lever that keeps re-consent rare is **naming categories of practice broadly enough that adding an allowlisted diagnostic field, switching cloud regions, or swapping AI vendors stays inside an already-disclosed purpose** ("we send your request to third-party AI providers to build your app" already covers a vendor swap; it would *not* cover a shift from "build your app" to "improve our AI models").

### (e) Do published privacy promises become legally enforceable — does over-promising create liability?

**High confidence, yes**, primarily through deception law rather than privacy law per se:

- **FTC Act §5 (US)**: the FTC's own framing, confirmed in guidance and case law, is that **privacy policies are treated as enforceable promises** — a company "must abide by them or face enforcement" for the "unfair or deceptive" practice of breaking a stated commitment. This is precisely the mechanism by which "we never send your data to X" becomes a legal liability if it later turns out to be false, independent of any privacy statute.
- **Canada**: PIPEDA's own model is complaint/investigation-driven around whether an org's actual practices match its stated ones (this is the whole point of Principle 8 "Openness" and Principle 3 "Consent" — a mismatch between promise and practice is the standard OPC finding pattern in complaint investigations, **medium confidence** on this being explicitly litigated as "deception" the way FTC frames it, since Canadian federal law doesn't have a direct §5 analog — the operative Canadian consumer-protection deception statute would be provincial, e.g. Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, not searched here — **flag for lawyer**). Quebec's Law 25 adds its own penalty regime (administrative monetary penalties, benchmarked GDPR-style against turnover) for privacy-practice violations specifically, which functions the same way: an unfulfilled written promise about data handling is itself the violation, not just "no privacy policy."
- **GDPR**: doesn't frame it as "deception," but Art. 5(1)(a) "lawfulness, fairness and transparency" plus the Art. 13 disclosure obligations mean a privacy notice that doesn't match actual practice is a **direct statutory violation** (not merely a broken promise) — arguably a stronger form of the same liability.

**Conclusion, directly answering the owner's principle**: the "promise everything Whim means to do, with room to spare, and nothing past that" strategy is not just good practice, it's **the specific thing that avoids FTC-style deception liability and GDPR transparency violations simultaneously** — an over-broad promise that later proves false ("nobody at Whim can read what you save") is exactly the fact pattern that turns a privacy policy into a lawsuit/enforcement target if it's ever untrue in practice (e.g., a future support-debugging feature that lets staff view saved data would retroactively falsify a categorical promise unless carved out from day one).

### (f) Other worldwide-solo-developer obligations, and size thresholds that exempt Whim

- **GDPR Art. 27 EU representative**: **High confidence** — required for any non-EU controller whose processing falls in scope under Art. 3(2) (targeting EU users), **unless** the narrow Art. 27(2) exemption applies: processing must be simultaneously (1) "occasional," (2) not large-scale special-category/criminal data, and (3) unlikely to create risk to data subjects — all three cumulative. Regulatory guidance interprets "occasional" narrowly, and commentary consulted says "most commercial digital services do not meet the occasional-processing criterion." **For a public, ongoing consumer app taking EU signups, the exemption is unlikely to apply** — a real representative appointment (a paid third-party service, typically) is the realistic expectation once EU availability goes live, not a size-based exemption. **No revenue/headcount threshold exists in GDPR itself** — it's a data-processing-scale test, not a company-size test, so being a solo developer does not exempt Whim.
- **GDPR territorial trigger (Art. 3(2))**: only applies if Whim is "offering goods or services" to EU-located individuals — mere technical accessibility isn't enough (Recital 23); intentionally listing the app for sale/download in EU countries on the Play/App Store **is** the kind of deliberate targeting that trips this (high confidence on the general legal test; medium on how a store's country-availability list specifically gets characterized by regulators, since I found no case squarely on "listed in the store" as the sole factor — but no serious commentator treats deliberate multi-country store availability as *not* targeting).
- **International transfers (GDPR Ch. V)**: since OpenRouter/model vendors are virtually certain to be outside the EU/EEA (and outside Canada), any EU user's prompt data routed through them needs a transfer mechanism — **Standard Contractual Clauses** (usable to any non-adequate country) or reliance on the **EU-US Data Privacy Framework** if a specific vendor is DPF-certified (US-only mechanism). A transfer risk assessment is required alongside SCCs. **Action item, not just doc language**: confirm each AI vendor actually reachable via OpenRouter's routing either self-certifies under the DPF or has SCCs in its own vendor terms — this is a genuine compliance gap risk if unconfirmed, not just a wording issue.
- **DPIA (GDPR Art. 35)**: required when processing, especially via "new technologies," is "likely to result in high risk" — EDPB guidance treats **LLM-based processing as an "innovative use" per se**, which "lowers the threshold" for other risk factors to trigger the requirement. **Medium confidence conclusion**: routing user prompts through third-party LLMs is close enough to this trigger that a documented (even lightweight) DPIA is a defensible, prudent step before/at EU launch, not just for large-scale operators — this is genuinely a place where "small solo developer" does **not** get a size exemption, since Art. 35's trigger is about processing *risk*, not company size.
- **PIPEDA**: applies to any organization in "commercial activity" involving Canadians' personal information — **no size/revenue threshold at all**. Extraterritorial reach confirmed by *Globe24h.com* (Federal Court applied PIPEDA to a Romanian company with zero Canadian presence, based on "real and substantial connection" — targeting/processing Canadians is enough). Since the developer *is* Canadian and Canadian users are certain, PIPEDA is the least avoidable regime here regardless of anything else.
- **Quebec's Law 25 — the most Whim-specific finding**: (1) **no size/revenue threshold** — applies to "any organization... carrying on an enterprise" that touches a Quebec resident's personal information; (2) requires designating a named **privacy officer** with published contact info (defaults to the highest-authority person absent written delegation — i.e., the owner, by default, today); (3) requires a **Privacy Impact Assessment before any communication of personal information outside Quebec**, assessing sensitivity, purpose, contractual/technical safeguards, and the destination jurisdiction's legal protections — this squarely covers **routing prompts to OpenRouter/US-based model vendors**, since Montreal-hosted servers sending data onward to US AI vendors is a transfer "outside Quebec" the moment it leaves the Montreal region's control. **This is a concrete, non-optional compliance step specific to being a Quebec/Canada-based operation** that a US-only developer wouldn't face in the same way, and it exists independent of Whim's small size.
- **CCPA/CPRA (California)**: **High confidence** — applies only if a business crosses one of three thresholds: >CAD-equivalent $25M (inflation-adjusted to ~US$26.625M for 2026) annual gross revenue, OR buys/sells/shares 100,000+ CA consumers'/households' personal information annually, OR derives ≥50% of revenue from selling/sharing personal information. **A solo-developer app is very unlikely to meet any of these at launch** — this is the one regime here where Whim's actual size plausibly earns a genuine, current exemption, though device-count could approach the 100k threshold at meaningful California-only scale (device IDs are almost certainly "personal information" under CCPA's broad definition, so if Whim's California install base alone crosses 100k devices/year, the threshold could be met even at near-zero revenue — worth monitoring by rough usage numbers, not revenue).
