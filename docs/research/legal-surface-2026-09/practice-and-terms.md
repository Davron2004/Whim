## Part A — Practice: how good products phrase this stuff

### A1. The core-promise pattern: absolute, short, implementation-free

The well-regarded privacy-first products all do the same three things: (1) lead with a one-line absolute claim, (2) state it as an architectural fact ("cannot," not "does not currently"), (3) never name the specific mechanism (no SDK names, no encryption algorithm, no server region) in the *promise* sentence — mechanism goes in a separate, expendable "how it works" section.

- **DuckDuckGo** — the entire policy opens with the promise, not a table of contents: *"We don't track you. That's our Privacy Policy in a nutshell."* (duckduckgo.com/privacy). Confidence: high (fetched directly).
- **Signal** — makes the promise about capability, not intent: *"Signal cannot decrypt or otherwise access the content of your messages or calls."* and separately, on monetization: *"Signal does not sell, rent or monetize your personal data or content in any way – ever."* (signal.org/legal). Confidence: high.
- **Obsidian** — ties the promise to a location fact that will stay true regardless of future features: *"All data is saved locally on your device and is never sent to our servers. We do not collect any personal data."* (obsidian.md/privacy). Confidence: high.
- **Standard Notes** — grounds the promise in the mechanism only insofar as it constrains the company, not the user's mental model: *"Because this data is end-to-end encrypted, we can't read it, and we can't sell it."* (standardnotes.com/privacy). Confidence: high.
- **Proton** — uses a minimization framing rather than a zero-collection one (Proton does run servers that see metadata): *"Our overriding policy is to collect as little user information (personal data included) as possible."* and, on content: *"We do not have the technical means to access the content of your encrypted emails, files, calendar events, passwords, or notes."* (proton.me/legal/privacy). Confidence: high. This is the closest analog to Whim's actual shape (a server exists; the promise is scoped to *what the server can see*, not "no server").
- **Bear** — one line, no caveats: *"Your notes never pass through our servers."* (bear.app, via blog/FAQ — could not fetch the primary privacy-policy page directly due to an iubenda iframe; confidence: medium, secondary-sourced).

**Pattern for Whim's wording:** the owner's draft promise ("what you save inside your apps never leaves your phone and nobody at Whim can read it") already matches this house style — it's a capability claim, not an intent claim, and it names no mechanism. Keep it that way. The place to watch is the *scope* of the noun: "what you save inside your apps" cleanly excludes the prompt text sent to generate the app in the first place, which does leave the phone (to OpenRouter). Signal and Proton both handle this kind of split by making the absolute promise only about the thing that is actually never transmitted (message *content* for Signal; note/mail *content* for Proton and Standard Notes) and describing everything else candidly nearby. Whim should do the same: keep the "never leaves your phone" promise scoped precisely to stored app data, and describe prompt transmission as its own, separately-labeled fact rather than letting it be read as covered by the same sentence.

### A2. Wording that survives growth without sounding evasive

The mechanism to widen without weakening: **promise the outcome, disclose the means separately, and make the means-section explicitly non-exhaustive/revisable.**

- Signal's "we don't sell... in any way – ever" is durable because it's a negative commercial promise (no monetization channel), which is cheap to keep true forever regardless of what infra changes.
- Proton's "as little ... as possible" is durable because it's a *standard*, not a *list* — new operational data (e.g., a new abuse-prevention signal) doesn't contradict a minimization standard the way it would contradict an enumerated "we only collect X, Y, Z."
- The failure mode to avoid is naming a vendor or mechanism inside a promise clause (e.g., "we use Sentry for crash reports" or "hosted in Montreal") — every one of the products above keeps vendor/infra facts either absent from the policy or in a clearly-labeled, separately-updatable subsection (Standard Notes' policy separately calls out that it avoids "industry standard tracking tools like Google Analytics" as a *practice statement*, not as part of the core promise). Whim's instinct to strip OpenRouter, GCP/Montreal, and specific SDK names out of the *promise* language and into an optional/expendable "current implementation" note is exactly this pattern. Confidence: high (directly observed across five independently-drafted policies).

### A3. How policy changes are handled: notice vs. re-consent, and how it's messaged

Two real patterns, sitting on either end of a spectrum:

- **"Continued use = acceptance" (deemed consent on notice), no re-click required.** Signal: *"We will update this privacy policy as needed so that it is current, accurate, and as clear as possible. Your continued use of our Services confirms your acceptance of our updated Privacy Policy."* Proton is nearly identical: *"Continued use of the Services after such changes are performed shall constitute your consent to it."* Obsidian's version puts the burden on the user to check back: *"you should check our website frequently to see recent changes."* DuckDuckGo: *"We will notify you of changes by posting the modified version on our website."* Confidence: high, all four fetched directly.
- **Advance-notice-for-material-change, with an explicit day count and delivery channel.** GitHub's privacy statement (public, version-controlled at github.com/github/site-policy — itself a practice worth naming, see below): *"GitHub may periodically revise this Privacy Statement. If there are material changes to the statement, we will provide at least 30 days prior notice by updating our website or sending an email to your primary email address associated with your GitHub account."* Confidence: high (fetched directly).
- **Version-controlled, diffable policy text as a trust mechanic.** GitHub and Basecamp (37signals) both publish their policies as public git repos (`github/site-policy`, `basecamp/policies`) so any change is a reviewable, dated diff, and Basecamp's founders have written publicly that they draft policies "by humans, for humans," in plain English, and invite public comment on the repo. Confidence: medium-high — the repos are directly verifiable; the "by humans for humans" framing is secondary-sourced (compli.com summary of DHH's public statements) but consistent with 37signals' well-known public communication style.
- **FTC's baseline expectation is notice-of-change **only when the change is non-adverse/consistent with the original promise**; a retroactive, undisclosed *material* change to how already-collected data is used is treated as deceptive even if a "we may change this policy" clause existed.** See A5/Part B(e) below (Gateway Learning). This is the legal floor under all the "continued use" clauses above — a company cannot use a buried change-clause to silently start doing something the original policy foreclosed.

**Pattern for Whim:** given the owner's stated goal (avoid the "your data now goes somewhere new" re-consent trigger), the practice precedent supports: (1) write the promise-level sentences broadly enough that *adding* an allowlisted crash-diagnostics stream, switching AI vendors, or adding an abuse-prevention signal are each **consistent with** — not contradictions of — the existing text, so they need only a changelog-style notice, not fresh consent; (2) reserve "this is a material change, here is 30 days notice" language for the one class of change that actually needs it under every framework surveyed: adding a **new category of recipient for user-identifying data**, or a **new purpose** not fairly encompassed by the original disclosure (see Part B(d)).

### A4. AI-vendor consent patterns

Apple's own ChatGPT/Siri integration is the most on-point real-world precedent, because it is structurally similar to Whim: a first-party app deciding whether/how to hand a user's request to a third-party model.

- **Default is a per-request confirmation prompt, not a one-time blanket consent:** *"You control when ChatGPT is used and will be asked before any of your information is shared."* Users can turn the confirmation off, **except** for files, which always prompt: *"Siri will always ask for your permission before sending any files to ChatGPT."* (apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/chatgpt-extension/, fetched directly). Confidence: high.
- **Precise disclosure of the payload sent**, itemized rather than vague: *"Your request and attachments... as well as limited data associated with the request, such as current time zone, country, device type, language, and feature being used... will be sent to ChatGPT."* Plus an explicit "what is NOT sent" carve-out for signed-out use: *"OpenAI will not receive any information tied to your Apple Account."* Confidence: high.
- **Explicit signpost that a different company's terms now govern:** *"When you are signed in, your ChatGPT account settings and OpenAI's data privacy policies will apply."* — Apple doesn't try to absorb OpenAI's practices into its own promise; it names the handoff. Confidence: high.
- **This is also now an Apple App Store rule, not just Apple's own practice** — see Part B(a)/(b): App Review Guideline 5.1.2 requires *every* app to do what Apple's own feature does: *"You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so."* (developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/, fetched directly, current text). Confidence: high — this is a live store rule Whim must satisfy, not just a style precedent.

**Pattern for Whim:** the store rule (5.1.2) plus Apple's own UX both point the same direction: disclose the AI hand-off plainly in the privacy policy (as a describe-the-flow paragraph — "the prompt you type to describe your mini-app is sent to third-party AI models we select to write its code"), and treat the *consent* as satisfied by the user's affirmative act of typing/speaking the request into a clearly-labeled generation flow, the way Apple treats Siri's per-request confirmation as the consent event rather than requiring a separate checkbox. A persistent settings-time notice ("mini-app requests are sent to AI providers to generate code") likely suffices as the "obtain explicit permission" step under 5.1.2 given the request itself is the only way the feature can work (cf. the "necessary for the service explicitly requested by the user" consent basis, Part B(b)) — but whether Apple's reviewers will accept notice-only vs. want a first-run interstitial is a store-behavior judgment, not a legal one. `[VERIFY: check current App Review acceptance pattern for comparable AI-generation apps before submission; guideline text alone doesn't establish reviewer practice.]`

### A5. Terms of Service: does Whim need its own, and which clauses matter

**Yes — a EULA (Apple's or Google's default) is not a substitute for a terms-of-service document**, because they solve different problems: a EULA is a *software license* (what the user may do with the binary); a ToS governs the *service relationship* (account-equivalent identity, acceptable use, content/output responsibility, liability, disputes). Whim has an ongoing server relationship (generation requests, device-ID ledger, reports) that a license grant doesn't touch at all.

- **What Apple's own minimum-terms EULA covers (and doesn't):** Apple requires developers' EULAs to include, verbatim in substance: *"You, not Apple, are solely responsible for the Licensed Application and the content thereof"*; a warranty disclaimer shifting all warranty liability to the developer; that the developer bears *"any claims of the End-User or any third party relating to the Licensed Application"*; that *"Apple, and Apple's subsidiaries, are third party beneficiaries of the EULA"* with the right to enforce it; and that the developer must supply contact information for end-user complaints. (apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/dev/minterms/, fetched directly.) Confidence: high. **Notice what's absent**: nothing here disclaims AI-output accuracy, sets a governing-law/forum, defines acceptable use, or addresses what happens when a user reports another user's mini-app. Those are exactly the gaps a small app's own ToS needs to fill.
- **AI-output disclaimer, real example (OpenAI's own Terms of Use):** *"Outputs may not always be accurate and may contain material inaccuracies even if they appear accurate because of their level of detail or specificity."* and *"Data and Output may be inaccurate, delayed, or outdated. Review important information and exercise independent professional judgment before relying on it."* (openai.com/policies — secondary-quoted by multiple trackers consistently; treat as medium-high confidence pending a direct refetch, but the phrasing is stable across sources and matches OpenAI's known house style.) This is the template clause Whim needs for its own generated code/apps: a disclaimer that AI-generated mini-app code may be wrong, non-functional, or behave unexpectedly, and that the user's reliance on it is at their own risk.
- **Clauses a small AI app's ToS realistically needs**, cross-referenced against what a bare EULA/Apple-Google defaults leave uncovered:
  - *Acceptable use* — what a user may not ask Whim to generate (the natural home for "reports" enforcement — reference the report mechanism here, not just in the privacy policy).
  - *AI-output/no-warranty disclaimer* — generated app code and generated-app behavior come with no correctness or fitness warranty (OpenAI-style clause above).
  - *Third-party AI processing acknowledgment* — cross-reference to the privacy policy's disclosure that prompts go to third-party model providers (keeps the ToS from re-stating and potentially drifting from the privacy policy's wording).
  - *Limitation of liability* — cap or exclude consequential damages; standard in essentially every ToS surveyed but not present in Apple's minimum EULA (which only allocates *Apple's* liability away, not Whim's exposure to users generally).
  - *Governing law / venue* — every real-world ToS names one explicitly (a small Canadian developer would typically name Ontario or Canada; this is a drafting choice, not a legal requirement to pick any particular forum, though enforceability of a chosen forum against a consumer varies by the user's own jurisdiction — `[VERIFY WITH COUNSEL: consumer-protection mandatory-forum rules in target markets, e.g. EU consumers generally cannot be forced out of their home-country courts regardless of a chosen-law clause]`).
  - *Termination/reports* — the ToS is the right place to reserve the right to act on a user report (remove a shared mini-app, suspend a device ID) since the report is described in the product as a ToS-enforcement mechanism, not a data-subject-rights mechanism.
- Confidence on "does a small app need its own ToS beyond the store defaults": high on the *practical* point (virtually every product surveyed — Signal, Proton, Standard Notes, Bear/Cultured Code, GitHub, Basecamp — layers its own ToS/EULA on top of, not instead of, store terms), medium on any claim that it's *legally mandatory* — no statute requires a ToS document to exist; the exposure is contractual/liability risk from having none, not a compliance violation. `[VERIFY WITH COUNSEL: whether Google Play's current developer policy affirmatively requires a ToS link for a free app with no purchases/no UGC-sharing, vs. merely recommending one — sources were inconsistent and none was primary Google Play policy text.]`

## Part B — Jurisdiction/scope answers (a)–(f)

**Governing frame:** Whim is a solo-operator business "carrying on an enterprise" in Canada (Ottawa/Ontario), so the floor is Canadian federal law — **PIPEDA** (Ontario has no general private-sector privacy statute of its own). Because the app is distributed to 176 Play countries and TestFlight globally, Whim likely also has to consider **Quebec's Law 25** (any Quebec resident-user triggers it, no threshold), and *may* trigger **GDPR** if EU users count as being "offered" the service (fact-dependent, see (f)). CCPA is very likely inapplicable given Whim's scale (see (f)). Apple and Google's *store* rules apply regardless of user geography, because they're contractual conditions of distribution, not law.

### (a) What MUST a privacy policy say, and in an exact form?

- **No statute reviewed prescribes exact wording.** PIPEDA, GDPR, and Law 25 all prescribe *required content*, not required phrasing — the operative legal test is comprehensibility/meaningfulness, not use of any magic words. Confidence: high.
- **PIPEDA (Schedule 1, Principle 4.8 Openness + Principle 4.3 Consent, s.6.1):** an organization must make readily available, in a form the individual can understand, information about its policies re: personal-information handling — what's collected, why, and with whom shared. The consent-validity test is explicit: *"the consent of an individual is only valid if it is reasonable to expect that an individual to whom the organization's activities are directed would understand the nature, purpose and consequences of the collection, use or disclosure."* (PIPEDA s.6.1, per priv.gc.ca guidance, quoted via OPC's own restatement — confidence high on substance, medium on exact statutory phrasing since fetched via secondary summary rather than the Justice Laws consolidated-statute text directly.)
- **GDPR Art. 13/14** *does* prescribe an exhaustive list of required disclosure items (if GDPR applies at all — see (f)): controller identity/contact, DPO contact if any, purposes + legal basis per purpose, legitimate-interest description where relied on, recipients/categories of recipients, international-transfer safeguards, retention period or criteria, the Art. 15–21 rights, right to withdraw consent, right to lodge a regulator complaint, whether provision is a legal/contractual requirement, and any automated-decision-making logic. Confidence: high (regulator/legal-guide consensus across multiple sources; not fetched from eur-lex.europa.eu text directly — `[VERIFY: pull Art. 13/14 text from eur-lex.europa.eu if GDPR applicability is confirmed]`).
- **Store rules are the binding "exact form" requirement in practice**, even though they aren't law: Apple 5.1.1 requires the policy to *"clearly and explicitly"* (a) identify what's collected/how/all uses, (b) confirm third parties get equal protection, (c) explain retention/deletion and how to revoke consent/request deletion (developer.apple.com, fetched directly, current text). Google Play's Data Safety section additionally requires the *in-Console declared* data practices to *match* the privacy-policy text — a consistency requirement that functions like a required-content rule even though it's not statutory (support.google.com/googleplay, secondary-sourced, medium confidence — could not fetch Play Console policy text directly).

### (c) Does anything regulate crash reporting or SDKs *as such*?

**No — every regime reviewed regulates the personal data an SDK happens to touch, not the SDK category itself.** Confidence: high, consistent finding across GDPR, PIPEDA, and FTC materials; no crash-reporting-specific statute found. Two qualifications:

1. **Store policy does single out SDKs by name as a disclosure trigger, even though law doesn't.** Google Play's Data Safety form requires disclosure of data collection *"including by any SDKs in your app"* and expanded in 2025 to require declaring device identifiers an SDK reads even incidentally (support.google.com, secondary-sourced, medium confidence). Apple 5.1.1 names *"analytics tools, advertising networks and third-party SDKs"* explicitly as the kind of third party whose data-protection-equivalence must be confirmed in the privacy policy (developer.apple.com, fetched directly, high confidence).
2. **ePrivacy Art. 5(3)'s reach was recently confirmed to extend to SDK reporting mechanics specifically** — EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 state the consent-for-storage/access rule covers *"local storage, SDK and connected-device reporting"* generally, not just classic cookies (edpb.europa.eu guideline PDF, secondary-quoted, medium-high confidence — the guideline itself is primary but was accessed via a summary rather than the PDF text directly). So an EU user's device, an SDK that reads/writes anything to local storage for crash reporting purposes needs to fit a consent-exemption (like the security/fraud-prevention one, itself narrower than commonly assumed) or needs consent — this is a live nuance if Whim ever adds a third-party crash SDK for EU-reachable users, `[VERIFY: read the EDPB PDF directly before relying on this]`.

### (d) When a policy changes: notice only, or fresh consent — and for which kinds of change?

- **GDPR:** consent, once given, cannot be stretched to cover a materially different purpose — *"there is no such thing as 'evolving' consent"* (ICO guidance, consistent with Recital 32/Art. 7 read together) — meaning a **new purpose** or **new category of recipient** that wasn't fairly disclosed originally needs **fresh, specific consent**, not just notice, wherever consent is the legal basis being relied on. Where the basis is legitimate interest or contract necessity rather than consent, the requirement is *updated disclosure*, not fresh consent, but the change still has to be communicated before or at the time it takes effect (Art. 13/14 obligations are ongoing, not one-time). Confidence: high on the principle, medium on precise sourcing (ICO summary, not GDPR text directly).
- **PIPEDA:** OPC's meaningful-consent guidance is explicit that organizations must **"obtain consent when making significant changes to privacy practices, including use of data for new purposes or disclosures to new third parties"** (priv.gc.ca, fetched via search result restating the guideline directly — confidence high). A **rewording** that doesn't change substance, or an **added purpose that's a natural extension already fairly implied** (the OPC guidance elsewhere distinguishes "integral" from "non-integral" purposes), does not require fresh consent — notice suffices.
- **FTC (U.S., relevant because store review and any U.S. users invoke it):** the enforcement pattern draws the line at **retroactive application to already-collected data** and **broken change-notice promises**, not change itself. *Gateway Learning* — the FTC's complaint targeted the company's failure to honor its own promise to notify users before applying a changed policy to previously-collected data; a **material, retroactive** policy change without the promised notice was the deceptive act (ftc.gov background, secondary-summarized, high confidence on the pattern, medium on case-citation precision — `[VERIFY: primary FTC complaint/consent order text for Gateway Learning, In re, before citing as precedent in anything client-facing]`).
- **Practice convergence:** the safest, growth-resistant design (per A3) is to write today's promise broadly enough that the anticipated additions (crash diagnostics, vendor switching, a failure code in the ledger) are **non-material clarifications of an already-disclosed purpose**, reserving "material change, N days notice" language for the one thing that would actually be a new purpose/new recipient class — e.g., beginning to share data for advertising, which the owner has already ruled out as a promise, or beginning to retain prompt content instead of discarding it after generation, if that's ever contemplated.

### (e) Do privacy promises become legally enforceable — does over-promising create liability?

**Yes, squarely, under U.S. FTC Act Section 5 and Canadian equivalents, and this is well-established, not a novel theory.**

- **FTC:** *"If a company makes privacy promises – either expressly or by implication – the FTC Act requires it to live up to those claims"*; failing to adhere to a privacy notice's own stated practices is treated as a deceptive trade practice (ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security, restated via search — confidence high on the doctrine, this is a decades-old, uncontroversial FTC position). Two illustrative cases: **Nomi Technologies** — FTC alleged deception because Nomi promised an opt-out mechanism at retail locations that it didn't actually provide, i.e., the *promise itself*, not any statute, created the obligation. **Gateway Learning** — deception based on breaking a *promise to notify before a policy change applied retroactively*. Both show the enforcement theory reaches **any gap between stated promise and actual practice**, regardless of whether the underlying data use would otherwise have been lawful. Confidence: high on the doctrine, medium on case specifics (secondary-sourced; `[VERIFY: primary FTC.gov case pages before citing specifics]`).
- **Canada:** PIPEDA's accountability and openness principles function similarly — an organization is bound by what its own published policy says it does, and the OPC can investigate a complaint that practice diverges from the stated policy. This is a structurally identical over-promising risk even without a U.S.-style "deception" doctrine. Confidence: medium (inferred from PIPEDA's structure; not backed by a specific enforcement case found in this research pass).
- **Direct implication for Whim's stated principle** ("promise everything Whim means to do, with room to spare — and nothing past that"): this is exactly the right instinct as a liability-avoidance strategy, not just a UX one — every extra word of specificity in the promise ("nobody at Whim can read it," "no cross-app tracking") becomes a claim the FTC-style doctrine could hold Whim to even in a jurisdiction (Canada) that lacks a named "deception" cause of action, because store reviewers and the OPC both read the policy the same literal way a regulator would.

### (f) Anything else a small non-US/non-EU solo developer shipping worldwide must do?

- **Named accountable individual (not optional, no size exemption):** PIPEDA requires *"every organization"* to designate an individual accountable for compliance, whose identity must be available on request (OPC guidance, confidence high — no small-business carve-out found). **Law 25 goes further**: the privacy officer role exists by default in the *CEO* unless delegated in writing, and — notably — **"there are no minimum thresholds... nor broad-brush exemptions... for categories of data or entities"** (BigID/legal-summary sourcing, confidence medium-high, consistent across multiple secondary sources but not fetched from the Quebec statute text directly). Practical read: Whim should name a specific person (title, e.g. "Privacy Officer") and contact address in the policy now, regardless of company size.
- **GDPR territorial reach is a genuine open question, not a settled "no":** GDPR Art. 3(2) reaches a non-EU controller that "offers... services" to EU-located data subjects, and EDPB guidance treats *availability in the EU app stores + language/localization choices* as relevant targeting factors — mere technical accessibility isn't enough, but Whim being live on Play across 176 countries (necessarily including EU member states) is at least a fact a regulator could point to. `[VERIFY WITH COUNSEL: whether Whim's actual store listings/languages amount to "offering services" to the EU under EDPB Guidelines 3/2018 — this is a judgment call this research cannot resolve.]` If GDPR does apply: **Art. 27 requires a designated EU representative** for non-EU controllers, and the "occasional processing" exemption is narrow — regulator commentary is blunt that *"very few organizations qualify... especially those operating online services or apps"* (gdprlocal.com et al., confidence medium — consistent secondary sourcing, not primary EDPB text). A **DPIA (Art. 35)** is likely *not* triggered on the facts described (no large-scale special-category data, no systematic public monitoring), but this should be revisited if Whim ever processes anything sensitive. Confidence: medium.
- **Breach notification is mandatory, not optional, under PIPEDA regardless of size:** the trigger is *"reasonable... to believe that the breach creates a real risk of significant harm"* (RROSH test) — if crossed, Whim must notify the OPC, affected individuals, and (in some cases) other organizations, **and must keep a record of every breach for two years regardless of whether the RROSH threshold is met** (priv.gc.ca, confidence high, fetched via direct guidance restatement).
- **CCPA is very likely inapplicable given Whim's actual scale** — the 2026 revenue threshold is ~US$26.6M/year and the consumer-count threshold is 100,000 CA residents/households; a solo-developer alpha app is almost certainly under both, and doesn't sell/share data for cross-context ads (which would trigger CCPA regardless of size in a narrower "data broker" sense — but Whim's no-ads/no-selling promise itself keeps it out of that door too). Confidence: medium-high — thresholds are clear and current; Whim's actual current user/revenue numbers weren't in scope for this research to confirm against them.
- **Anonymous device ID ≠ Apple's "tracking" for ATT purposes**, as long as it's never linked/combined with data from other companies' apps/sites for ads/measurement — Apple's own definition of tracking is specifically that cross-company linkage, not device-scoped rate-limiting (developer docs, confidence high on the definition, this is Apple's stated, stable definition). This means Whim's device-ID ledger design, as described, should **not** require an App Tracking Transparency prompt — but this is worth re-confirming at implementation time since Apple's review can be stricter in practice than its documentation. `[VERIFY: at build/submission time, not from this doc alone.]`
