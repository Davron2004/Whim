# Whim legal surface — EU/UK + US (GitHub issue #63, research date 2026-09-23)

**Not legal advice. This is research for an attorney to check.** Confidence tags: (H)=high, (M)=medium, (L)=low — low means "verify with counsel before relying on it." Every quote below is copied verbatim from the source at the linked URL; paraphrases are marked as such.

**Facts assumed** (from the issue): solo dev, AnyCognition, Ottawa, anycognition.ca. iOS (TestFlight) + Android (Play alpha, 176 countries selected), heading to public release worldwide. No accounts. Anonymous device ID for daily limits + cost ledger. User prompts (voice/text) go to Whim's server (GCP `northamerica-northeast1`, Montreal) which calls third-party AI model providers through OpenRouter to generate mini-app code. Mini-apps run on-device; user-saved app data never leaves the phone. Users can send an app report.

## 0. The one fact that reframes everything: Apple already regulates this by contract

Before GDPR or the AI Act: **Apple App Store Review Guideline 5.1.2(i)**, updated 2025-11-13, is a live, binding gate on shipping at all (H). Verbatim, from developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ (`WebFetch`, 2026-09-23):

> "(i) Unless otherwise permitted by law, you may not use, transmit, or share someone's personal data without first obtaining their permission. You must provide access to information about how and where the data will be used. **You must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, including with third-party AI, and obtain explicit permission before doing so.**"

Practitioner commentary (TechCrunch 2025-11-13, `stora.sh` implementation guide, `dev.to` writeup — (M), secondary sources but consistent and dated close to the rule) says enforcement wants the **actual provider name** in the disclosure and consent flow ("OpenAI", "Anthropic", etc.) — "Generic 'Some features use AI' gets rejected." Whim routes through OpenRouter to *multiple, swappable* model vendors, which is in tension with naming a specific provider. Two consistent paths: (i) disclose "your prompt is sent to third-party AI providers we select and may change (e.g. via OpenRouter) to generate your app," naming OpenRouter as the routing layer plus a representative list, not a single frozen vendor name, and get one explicit permission before the first prompt is ever sent — this is also what GDPR needs anyway (§2 below); or (ii) treat it as a reviewer-risk item to pressure-test against a current rejected/accepted app before public release. This is a **platform contract term, not law**, but it is the thing most likely to actually block a release, so it belongs in the same document as the legal analysis. (M — verify against current App Review outcomes closer to ship, since enforcement patterns move.)

Google Play's Data Safety section requires declared, accurate data-collection/sharing disclosures with consent gating "before" collection for sensitive categories (Play Console Help, `support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311` — (M), not independently re-verified this session against primary text) — less prescriptive about naming AI sub-processors than Apple's rule, but the same "disclose before sending" posture applies.

## 1. EU/UK — does this reach Whim at all, and how

### 1.1 Territorial scope

**GDPR Art. 3(2)** (gdpr-info.eu/art-3-gdpr, and EDPB Guidelines 3/2018 on territorial scope — (H)): applies to a non-EU controller when processing relates to "the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment ... is required, to such data subjects in the Union" or "the monitoring of their behaviour." A free, worldwide-distributed consumer app that Whim actively lists for and serves to EU users is "offering services" — mere accessibility isn't enough, but active distribution via the EU/UK App Store and Play storefronts is (Wiley, GDPR.eu — (H) as applied to an app deliberately available in-country, consistent across every source pulled). **Conclusion: GDPR applies to any personal data of EU-located users the moment Whim serves them from an EU-facing store listing.** Same logic, same conclusion, for **UK GDPR** (near-identical Art. 3(2) wording via the Data Protection Act 2018 saved-law regime).

**Art. 27 EU representative / UK GDPR Art. 27 UK representative**: mandatory for an Art. 3(2)-scoped non-EU/non-UK controller **unless** processing is "occasional," not large-scale special-category data, and low-risk (gdpr-info.eu/art-27-gdpr — (H) for text; practitioner sources agree the exemption is read narrowly). A public consumer app with continuous EU/UK user traffic is not "occasional" processing by any EDPB-consistent reading found (multiple sources concur: "very few organizations qualify... especially those operating online services or apps" — (M), synthesis of secondary sources, but directionally consistent with EDPB Guidelines 3/2018 which frames "occasional" as incidental to non-regular activity). **Practical read: once EU/UK users are non-trivial, Whim needs both an EU Art. 27 representative and a separate UK Art. 27 representative** (they cannot be the same entity/uncombined without care — commonly a paid representative service, not counsel). (M — the "occasional" call is fact-sensitive; get counsel to confirm rather than assume, especially before or shortly after EU/UK public launch.)

**EU AI Act Art. 2** territorial scope (artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/2 — (M), synthesized from secondary sources, not the primary Regulation text itself this session): applies to non-EU providers/deployers "where the output produced by the [AI] system is used in the Union." Whim's server-side AI is a GPAI-consumption pipeline generating app code that then runs on an EU user's phone — arguably "output ... used in the Union." (L on whether regulators would in practice treat a code-generation backend this way vs. treating the mini-app SDK boundary as the relevant system; this is a real open question, not settled by anything found.)

### 1.2 (a) What must a privacy notice say — is any of it in exact form?

**GDPR Art. 13** (gdpr-info.eu/art-13-gdpr — (H) for text) is the controlling checklist when data is collected directly from the user (this is Whim's case — no third-party data brokers). It requires, verbatim per Art. 13(1)-(2): identity/contact of the controller; DPO contact "where applicable"; **"the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended as well as the legal basis for the processing"**; where processing is based on Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests, "the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party"; recipients or categories of recipients; intention to transfer to a third country and the safeguard relied on; **the retention period "or if that is not possible, the criteria used to determine that period"**; existence of the rights to access/rectify/erase/restrict/object/portability; the right to withdraw consent; the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority; whether provision of data is a statutory/contractual requirement and consequences of not providing it; and existence of automated decision-making/profiling with meaningful logic if applicable. **No exact wording is mandated** — the standard is Art. 12(1): "concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language." (H)

**PECR reg. 6 / ePrivacy Art. 5(3)** layers a *separate* notice-plus-consent requirement that is not about "personal data" at all — it is about **access to information stored on the device**, so it applies even to non-personal, non-identifying data (see §1.3). ICO guidance (`ico.org.uk/.../how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-rules/` — (H)) requires clear information about what is being stored/accessed and why, before that storage/access happens, as a precondition of valid consent.

**AI Act Art. 50(5)** (artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50 — (M), via `WebFetch` summary, not primary-text verbatim): the interaction-disclosure duty (§1.4 below) must be given "in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction," so *timing* is prescribed even though wording isn't.

**No EU/UK law found here requires a specific verbatim disclosure sentence** — the closest thing to an "exact form" requirement is Apple's own rule (§0), which is contractual, not statutory.

### 1.3 (b) What needs opt-in consent vs. another legal basis

GDPR Art. 6(1) legal bases, verbatim (gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr — (H)): (a) consent; (b) "necessary for the performance of a contract... or to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract"; (c) legal obligation; (d) vital interests; (e) public task; (f) "necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller ... except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject." Consent under Art. 7 must be freely given/specific/informed/unambiguous, and Art. 7(3) (gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr — (H)): **"The data subject shall have the right to withdraw his or her consent at any time... It shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent."**

Applied per data flow:

- **User prompts → third-party AI providers.** This is the core, requested functionality — a user who types "build me a tip calculator" is asking, by definition, for that prompt to be turned into code. **Art. 6(1)(b) (contract/pre-contractual necessity)** is the natural GDPR basis if Whim's terms-of-use function as the "contract," even absent a signup — EDPB and most DPA guidance accept "necessary to provide the specific service the user affirmatively requested" as 6(1)(b)-eligible for the *processing itself*. **But this is a third-country transfer, not just a legal-basis question** (see §1.6) — a lawful Art. 6 basis does **not** by itself authorize sending data to a US-based model vendor; Art. 44+ safeguards are additionally required. **Apple's 5.1.2(i) independently requires explicit opt-in consent before the first send, regardless of what GDPR basis you pick** (§0) — so in practice Whim will implement an explicit consent gate for app-store reasons even if GDPR alone would tolerate 6(1)(b). (M) Recommendation: satisfy the stricter (Apple) bar and treat it as covering the GDPR bar too, rather than relying on two different postures.

- **Anonymous device ID for rate limits / cost ledger.** Two separate regimes stack:
  - *GDPR*: a device ID is very likely still "personal data" (singles out a device/session even without a name — Breyer-line reasoning; ICO guidance treats persistent device identifiers as personal data when they can be used to single someone out, even pseudonymously). **Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests** is the fitting basis (fraud/abuse prevention and cost control are textbook legitimate interests; GDPR Recital 47 explicitly names "preventing fraud" as an example legitimate interest) — **not** consent, provided a documented legitimate-interests balancing test (LIA) is on file and the ID is not repurposed for anything else. (M)
  - *ePrivacy/PECR* (device-storage-access layer, separate from GDPR): Art. 5(3) verbatim (via ConsentTheater/Deceptive Patterns compilations, cross-checked — (M) for the *quote's* exactness since I could not load the official OJ text this session, but it is the standard, widely-reproduced text): "This shall not prevent any technical storage or access for the sole purpose of carrying out the transmission of a communication... **or as strictly necessary in order for the provider of an information society service explicitly requested by the subscriber or user to provide that service.**" ICO's own framing of the "strictly necessary" exemption explicitly includes **"preventing or detecting fraud in connection with providing the service"** as strictly-necessary, and separately flags that the assessment must be from **the user's point of view**, and that a purpose used for anything beyond the stated strictly-necessary purpose forfeits the exemption (`ico.org.uk/.../what-are-the-exceptions/` — (H)). Rate-limiting/abuse-prevention tied to the service the user is actively using is a strong fit for the strictly-necessary exemption; **cost-ledger analytics repurposing the same ID for anything beyond that (e.g. product analytics) would not be**, and would need consent instead. **Practical rule: keep the device ID single-purpose (abuse/rate-limit/cost) or split it into two IDs with two different bases.** (M)

- **Server operational/security logs.** Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interest (network/information security) is well-settled — GDPR Recital 49 explicitly: processing "to the extent strictly necessary and proportionate ... to ensure network and information security" is a legitimate interest. No consent needed if scoped to security/operations and normal retention limits. (H)

- **Allowlisted crash/error diagnostics not linked to identity.** This is the least settled item found. If genuinely de-identified and allowlisted (no persistent ID, no linkage to the device/rate-limit ID), GDPR treatment is straightforward (Art. 6(1)(f), or arguably outside GDPR scope entirely if truly anonymous per Art. 26 GDPR/Recital 26 anonymization standard — but "allowlisted fields, not linked to identity" is not automatically "anonymous" under the strict GDPR test, which asks whether *anyone* could re-identify, not just whether Whim intends to). **The PECR/ePrivacy layer is the sharper issue**: EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 (adopted 2024-10-16, `edpb.europa.eu` — (H) for adoption date and general scope) read Art. 5(3) broadly to cover "certain local processing" and reading/writing device information generally, **not limited to advertising cookies** — a crash SDK that reads/writes local storage to collect and transmit a crash report is doing exactly the kind of "access to information stored in terminal equipment" Art. 5(3) governs, independent of whether the payload is "personal data." Whether it qualifies for the "strictly necessary for the service requested" exemption is genuinely arguable both ways: an app crashing and Whim fixing it arguably serves the user, but EDPB's consistent posture (and the ICO's 2026-04-29 updated cookie guidance, `cookieyes.com`/`didomi.io` — (M)) is that the exemption is read **narrowly** and is assessed **from the user's perspective at the moment of the specific storage/access**, not "does this benefit the product long-term." Analytics-adjacent uses are the standard example of something DPAs say needs consent even when the operator thinks it's "just for quality." **Recommendation: do not assume crash diagnostics are consent-free; either (i) gate them behind the same one-time consent screen as the AI-send disclosure (bundling is fine since both are "how the app works" disclosures, per Apple's own combined consent pattern), or (ii) get a documented case for the strictly-necessary exemption before relying on it.** (M — this is the single most litigable/enforcement-risk item in the whole EU/UK analysis; flag explicitly for counsel.)

- **User-sent reports.** User-initiated, so **Art. 6(1)(b)** (the user is requesting an action: "review this app") is clean, and no PECR issue arises beyond what's already disclosed, since the user is the one triggering the send. (H)

### 1.4 (c) Is anything regulated "as such" (the SDK/mechanism), or only the personal data?

**Yes — this is the one place EU/UK law regulates the *mechanism*, not the data.** ePrivacy Art. 5(3) / PECR reg. 6 apply to "storage of information, or ... gaining access to information already stored, in the terminal equipment," full stop — EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 confirm this is technology-scoped (cookies, SDKs, local storage, device fingerprinting, "certain local processing," IoT reporting) and **explicitly states the concept of "information" covers both personal and non-personal data** (`edpb.europa.eu` guidelines summary — (H)). So a crash-reporting SDK or any local-storage-reading library is regulated by ePrivacy/PECR *by virtue of what it technically does*, independent of GDPR's personal-data threshold. GDPR itself is data-scoped, not mechanism-scoped — it doesn't care whether the mechanism is an SDK, a server call, or a spreadsheet, only whether personal data flows.

The EU AI Act likewise regulates certain **systems as such** (Art. 50's chatbot-disclosure and synthetic-content-marking duties attach to "AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons" and to "providers of AI systems ... generating synthetic ... text content" — artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50, (M) via secondary synthesis) rather than to personal data. **Applied to Whim**: the voice/text interface where a user describes a mini-app to the AI is plausibly "an AI system intended to interact directly with natural persons" under Art. 50(1), triggering a duty to disclose that the user is interacting with AI — **unless "it is obvious from ... the circumstances of use"** (the same source's paraphrase of the built-in exception). Given Whim's entire premise is "describe an app and AI builds it," obviousness is a defensible position, but a one-line "Whim uses AI to build your app" disclosure at first use is cheap insurance and matches the owner's "promise with room to spare" instinct rather than betting on the obviousness exception. **Whether AI-generated *code* counts as "synthetic text content" under Art. 50(2)'s machine-readable-marking duty is not resolved by anything found this session** — the marking duty's usual examples (deepfakes, AI-written articles) are about *content shown to third parties as if human-made*; generated app source code, executed rather than published/read as prose, is a different fact pattern nothing found directly addresses. (L — flag for counsel specifically; do not assume either way.) Article 50 has applied since **2026-08-02**, with generative systems already on the market before that date given until **2026-12-02** for the machine-readable marking piece specifically (Sidley `datamatters.sidley.com`, Cloud Security Alliance research note — (H) for the two dates, both independently corroborated).

### 1.5 (d) Policy changes: notice only, or fresh consent?

Depends on *what* changed, and this maps directly onto the owner's "why it matters" concern:

- **GDPR**: Any change that is **consent-based** and expands scope — "new data types, new purposes, new recipients" — needs **fresh, separate consent**, not just notice; EDPB's consent guidance is explicit that each distinct processing purpose needs its own consent, and a controller "cannot simply rely on" the original consent for a materially different purpose (Usercentrics/activeMind summaries of EDPB Guidelines on consent — (M), secondary synthesis, directionally consistent with the EDPB's published Guidelines 05/2020 on consent, not independently re-pulled this session). **Rewording for clarity that doesn't change scope does not require re-consent** — this is the important other half: Whim can clarify or reorganize its privacy text, or *narrow* what it does, on notice alone. For data processed under **Art. 6(1)(f) legitimate interests** (device ID, logs), a new purpose needs a fresh LIA and Art. 13 notice update, not "consent" as such (there's no consent to withdraw), but a materially adverse new use could tip the LIA balancing test against the controller.
- **PECR/ePrivacy**: consent for a storage/access technology is purpose-specific (§1.3) — adding a new purpose for the same technology (e.g., repurposing the abuse-prevention device ID for product analytics) is not covered by the original consent/exemption and needs new consent for that new purpose, regardless of whether GDPR consent was even the operative basis.
- **AI Act Art. 50** disclosures are ongoing/point-in-time obligations (disclose at time of interaction), not "consented-to" facts that can go stale in the same way — a change here is a product-behavior compliance question, not a re-consent question.

**Net for the owner's "every text change forces re-agreement" worry**: it is **true for consent-based items and false for notice-based (legitimate-interest) items** — this is exactly why the recommendation across this whole document is to put as much as legally possible (server logs, security, abuse-prevention device ID) on **legitimate interest**, not consent, and reserve actual consent screens only for the AI-prompt-sharing item Apple forces anyway (§0) and possibly crash diagnostics (§1.3). A consent-gated item is the one where future *scope* changes (new purpose, new recipient, new data category) cost a re-consent flow; a legitimate-interest item costs only an updated Art. 13 notice, which does not require the user to click anything.

### 1.6 (e) Do privacy promises become legally enforceable in the EU/UK?

Not primarily through a US-style "deception" doctrine, but functionally yes, through several EU/UK mechanisms: (1) **Accountability principle, Art. 5(2) GDPR** — the controller must be able to **demonstrate** compliance with what it told users, so an inaccurate Art. 13 notice is itself a GDPR infringement (transparency principle, Art. 5(1)(a)/12/13), enforceable by DPAs up to the **Art. 83(5) fine tier: up to €20m or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher** (gdpr-info.eu — (H), standard, widely known figure, not independently re-quoted from the OJ text this session but not in dispute anywhere). (2) The **EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive** and UK equivalent (Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024) prohibit misleading commercial claims generally, which would reach an over-promised privacy claim used to market the app. **Not independently verified this session against primary UCPD/DMCC text — flag for counsel (M/L).**

### 1.7 (f) Other EU/UK-specific musts and possible exemptions

- **Art. 27 representatives (EU and UK, separately)** — see §1.1; the "occasional processing" exemption is a narrow needle Whim likely doesn't thread once EU/UK traffic is non-trivial. (M)
- **UK data-protection fee / ICO registration**: UK GDPR/DPA 2018 requires most controllers processing personal data to pay the ICO's data protection fee and register, subject to small exemptions not evidently applicable to a public consumer app — **not independently re-verified against the current ICO fee-notice text this session; flag for counsel** (L, but this is a cheap, easy compliance item worth just doing rather than researching further).
- **Transfers, Art. 44+**: OpenRouter and most frontier model vendors are US-domiciled. As of 2026-09, **the EU-US Data Privacy Framework remains valid law** (2023 Commission adequacy decision upheld by the EU General Court, 2025-09-03, Case T-553/23, on appeal to the CJEU; a "Schrems III" NOYB challenge is pending; commentators expect a CJEU ruling "by late 2026 or early 2027" and flag real invalidation risk given the loss of PCLOB quorum in Jan 2025 — DLA Piper, IAPP, activeMind, Freshfields — (M), consistent across four independent secondary sources, but this is a live, moving target). **Practical implication: if the model vendor(s) Whim uses are DPF-certified, that is currently sufficient for the transfer leg; if not, Standard Contractual Clauses are the fallback; either way this needs to be revisited if/when the CJEU rules, since it could evaporate GDPR transfer cover overnight the way Schrems II did to Privacy Shield.** Do not hard-code "DPF-certified" as a permanent architectural assumption — this is exactly the kind of "implementation detail that shouldn't leak into the consent text" the owner's principle warns about; the *promise* to users should be about safeguards existing, not naming DPF by name.
- **Canada-EU adequacy (relevant to the Montreal server, not the AI leg)**: Canada/PIPEDA retains its adequacy decision, reconfirmed in the Commission's January 2024 first-review report, but **the adequacy decision covers only recipients subject to PIPEDA** — commercial organizations, which a Canadian solo-dev consumer app operator plausibly is (Lexology, Dentons, WeirFoulds — (M), consistent across sources; PIPEDA's federal replacement bill C-27 died on prorogation Jan 2025 with no replacement as of mid-2026, so PIPEDA itself, not a successor, is the operative Canadian law). This means EU-to-Montreal transfers (the server itself) are **not** the transfer problem — it's the Montreal-to-US-AI-vendor leg that needs Art. 44+ cover.
- **DPIA (Art. 35)**: not clearly triggered on the facts given (no large-scale special-category data, no systematic large-scale public monitoring) but a documented threshold assessment is good practice given the AI-processing angle; not independently resolved this session (L).

## 2. United States

### 2.1 FTC Act §5 — the backbone, and it has no size floor

FTC's own framing (`ftc.gov` topic page — (H)): Section 5 reaches "unfair or deceptive acts or practices," and this has covered "broken privacy promises, bad data security, and dark patterns for over two decades." Recent enforcement in-window (`ftc.gov` press releases, cited via search — (H) for existence/dates, not independently re-fetched from ftc.gov this session): **FTC and States Act Against Hims & Hers for Deceptive and Unlawful Privacy Practices** (2026-07-29); Cox Media Group settlement over deceptive "active listening" AI marketing claims (2026-08-27). The operative test practitioners describe: "what companies promised consumers and whether actual practices match" — a privacy policy claim that's false is itself the violation, independent of any state-law threshold. **This is the single most important reason to follow the owner's "promise nothing past what's true" principle**: FTC jurisdiction has no revenue or user-count floor the way state privacy statutes do. (H)

**Retroactive/material changes**: *In re Gateway Learning* (2004, `ftc.gov`, Wikipedia summary — (H)) is the founding precedent: Gateway Learning was barred from **"applying material changes in its privacy policy retroactively, without consumers' consent,"** and specifically from using previously-collected data under a new, more permissive policy without **express affirmative ("opt-in") consent**. This principle was later "extended to include retroactive changes to privacy settings in the FTC's case against Facebook" (Lexology/Lawoffice summaries — (M)). **Applied to Whim: a future privacy-policy change that newly permits something not covered by what users agreed to when their data was originally collected requires opt-in consent for the retroactive scope, not just notice — this is the FTC-law analogue of the GDPR consent-scope point in §1.5, and both point the same direction.** Recent Loeb & Loeb commentary specifically flags this in the AI context — "Retroactive Privacy Policy Updates Require Consent" when repurposing consumer data for new AI uses (`quicktakes.loeb.com` — (M)).

### 2.2 CalOPPA — applies now, no threshold, easy to satisfy

CalOPPA covers any operator collecting PII from California residents through a website or online app — **no revenue or user-count threshold found** in any source pulled (all sources describe it as applying broadly to "operators," not gated by size — (M), consistent absence of a threshold across all 10 sources, though I did not find a single primary-statute citation stating "no threshold" affirmatively, so treat as M not H). Required content (iubenda, Termly, CookieYes — (M), consistent across independent secondary summaries): **an effective date; categories of PII collected; categories of third parties data is shared with; a description of the process (if any) for a user to review/request changes to their data; a description of how the operator notifies users of policy changes; and, per the 2013 AB 370 amendment (effective 2014-01-01), a disclosure of "how the operator responds to Do Not Track signals"** — the law does not require honoring DNT, only disclosing the practice. Whim's "no cross-app tracking" promise makes the DNT disclosure trivial to write honestly ("we do not track you across apps or sites, so Do Not Track doesn't apply to anything we do"). **This is a must-do, low-cost, low-controversy item — there's no reason not to satisfy it exactly, in full, on day one.** (M)

### 2.3 CCPA/CPRA and the rest of the US state patchwork — thresholds, watch them, don't assume permanent exemption

CCPA/CPRA thresholds for 2026 (Transcend, Clym, Jackson Lewis — (M), consistent figures across sources, inflation-adjusted number specifically flagged as such): a business is covered if **any** of: annual gross revenue over **$26,625,000** (2026 inflation-adjusted from the original $25M); **buys, sells, or shares personal information of 100,000+ CA consumers or households annually**; or **derives 50%+ of annual revenue from selling/sharing personal information**. A free app with no revenue and no ad-based data sale is unlikely to hit the revenue or revenue-share prongs, but **the 100,000-consumer/household threshold is a real, live risk given Whim's stated ambition ("public release," 176 countries selected on Play) — 100k CA users alone is a plausible outcome of a successful launch, and the count is "consumers or households," not "paying customers."** (M — this is a threshold to monitor with real telemetry once public, not something to assume away permanently.) Other 2026 state comprehensive privacy laws (Virginia, Connecticut: 100,000-consumer threshold, dropping to 25,000/25% if data-sale revenue is involved for CT, 50% for VA; Colorado: 100,000, or 25,000 with data-sale revenue; Utah: Virginia-style consumer threshold **plus** its own >50%-revenue-from-sale-of-PI-and-25,000-consumers test — feroot, enzuzo, clym, zerodaylaw — (M), consistent structure across five independent trackers though none independently cross-checked against a state's own statute text this session) follow the same basic shape: **100,000 consumers is the single most-repeated tripwire number across the entire US state patchwork.** Recommendation: build the privacy notice to already say everything CCPA/VCDPA-style laws would require (categories collected, purposes, no sale, deletion-request mechanism even if not legally required yet) — cheap now, and removes the "which of 20 states just started applying to us" problem entirely, consistent with the owner's "room to spare" principle.

### 2.4 COPPA

FTC's own framing (`ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-not-just-kids-sites` — (H)): COPPA applies automatically to **child-directed** services; for a **general-audience** service, it applies only where the operator has **"actual knowledge"** a specific user is under 13. Whim has no accounts, no age field, no birthdate collection, and nothing in the facts given suggests child-directed design or content — **so COPPA's core parental-consent/notice obligations are very unlikely to bite unless Whim collects something that creates "actual knowledge"** (e.g., a report or support message that says "I'm 10"). A 2026-02-25 FTC Policy Statement (`ftc.gov` press release — (H) for date/existence) separately **exempts mixed-audience/general-audience operators with actual knowledge of under-13 users from needing parental consent solely to run age-verification technology**, if data is limited to age-determination, promptly deleted, etc. — relevant only if Whim ever adds age-gating, not otherwise. **Recommendation: keep it this way — no age field, no birthdate, no child-directed marketing — and COPPA stays out of scope by design, which is exactly the kind of "don't create the trigger" move the owner's principle rewards.** (M — "actual knowledge" is a fact-specific standard; a report inbox that a human reads and might see a child's self-disclosed age is a real, if low-probability, path to actual knowledge; a documented "we do not read reports for age signals and do not act on them" policy is not a real fix if a human actually reads and acts on them.)

### 2.5 Does US law regulate crash SDKs/AI SDKs "as such"?

**Not found.** Every US mechanism located here (FTC, CalOPPA, CCPA/CPRA, COPPA) is data/practice-scoped, not technology-scoped — the US has nothing equivalent to ePrivacy Art. 5(3)'s device-access trigger. The nearest thing is CCPA/CPRA's broad "sale/share" definitions capturing cross-context ad-tech SDKs specifically **when they enable cross-context behavioral advertising** — irrelevant here since Whim runs no ads and does no cross-app tracking by design. (M)

## 3. Consolidated recommendations against the owner's principle

The owner's rule — "promise everything Whim means to do, with room to spare, and nothing past that" — maps cleanly onto the legal-basis choice in §1.3/§1.5 and the FTC point in §2.1:

1. **Never write an implementation-specific promise into user-facing text.** "We use OpenRouter" or "we use Firebase Crashlytics" are exactly the sentences that force re-consent (GDPR) or re-disclosure risk (FTC) the moment the vendor changes; "we send your request to third-party AI providers, which may change" and "we collect limited crash diagnostics not linked to your identity" survive vendor swaps without triggering either regime's change-notice machinery. Apple's naming requirement (§0) is the one place a *specific* name may be needed in a *consent screen*, but that's a narrower, more disposable surface than the privacy policy itself — keep them separate documents/screens.
2. **Split consent-gated items from legitimate-interest items deliberately**, because only the consent-gated bucket forces re-agreement on scope changes (§1.5): put the AI-prompt-send behind one clear opt-in (satisfies GDPR, PECR-adjacent reasoning, and Apple 5.1.2(i) simultaneously); put device-ID-for-abuse-prevention and server logs on legitimate interest/strictly-necessary with no consent screen at all, and *keep them single-purpose* so they don't drift out of their exemption.
3. **Crash diagnostics is the one genuinely unresolved item** (§1.3, §1.4) — decide with counsel whether to fold it into the same one-time consent screen as the AI-send (cheap, safe, matches "room to spare") or build a documented strictly-necessary case; do not silently assume it's consent-free.
4. **Treat CCPA/state-law and Apple/Google disclosure formats as the de facto global baseline** even before thresholds are legally crossed (§2.3) — it's nearly free to write the notice to already satisfy the strictest applicable format, and it means growth past a 100k-user threshold in any one state never forces a scramble.
5. **Get an EU and a UK Art. 27 representative lined up before or immediately at EU/UK public launch** (§1.1) — this is a real, distinct, non-optional cost item that a lot of solo-dev launches miss.
6. **Revisit the international-transfer mechanism (EU-US DPF) on a standing reminder, not a one-time check** (§1.7) — it is under active litigation and could be invalidated with little warning, the way Privacy Shield was.

## 4. Open questions to put in front of a lawyer specifically

- Does "occasional processing" genuinely exempt Whim from Art. 27 pre-launch, or only once EU/UK volume is established? (§1.1)
- Is server-side AI-code-generation "output ... used in the Union" under AI Act Art. 2, or is the relevant "AI system" only the on-device mini-app (which arguably isn't an AI system at all once generated)? (§1.1, §1.4)
- Does AI Act Art. 50(2)'s synthetic-content marking duty reach generated *source code* at all, or only human-consumed prose/media? (§1.4)
- Is the crash-diagnostics SDK covered by PECR's strictly-necessary exemption, or does it need its own consent gate? (§1.3)
- Is a UK ICO data-protection-fee registration currently required, and is there any small-operator exemption Whim could claim? (§1.7)
- Should "device ID for rate limiting" and "device ID for cost ledger" be architecturally split now, before any drift toward a second, less-defensible use makes the single-ID legitimate-interest/strictly-necessary case harder to defend later? (§1.3)
- Does a human ever reading user-submitted app reports create COPPA "actual knowledge" exposure, and does the report-handling process need a documented no-action-on-inferred-age policy? (§2.4)
