01 · The argument
Whim's interface is mostly prose. So the type system is the design system.

Almost every screen in Whim is a machine talking: what it plans to do, what it is doing, what it did, why it failed. There are very few controls. That makes this product much closer to a reading interface than to a normal app, and it means the usual design-system output — a button inventory — is the least valuable part of the work.

The two decisions that actually determine whether Whim feels good are how that prose is voiced and how it is typeset. Everything in section 04 and 05 is that. The rest is housekeeping.

03 · Type
Three faces: one speaks, one measures, one quotes.

Space Grotesk is retired. It is the default pick of a popular design tool, which makes it a tell — and it will keep getting more prevalent, not less. Instrument Sans replaces it: same plain, low-quirk temperament, slightly narrower, and unlike Grotesk it ships a real italic, so faked shear is never a risk anywhere in the product.

The third face is now confirmed: Newsreader italic, for quoted user text only. It never appears upright, never in a heading, never for anything the system wrote. Its whole job is that the user's own words look different in kind, not merely in colour.

04 · Whim Syntax
Highlighting prose by what a word does in the product — not by what part of speech it is.

The instinct is right: syntax colouring makes code scannable, and Whim's messages are read the way code is read — skimmed for the one thing that changed. But an IDE does not colour by grammar. It colours by role in the system: this is a literal, this is a name, this is a keyword. Grammatical colouring — nouns blue, verbs red — colours everything, and colouring everything is colouring nothing.

So the proposal keeps the mechanism and changes the vocabulary. Six token classes, drawn from what actually varies in a Whim sentence.

PROPOSEDThe brown is not a quote colour. It is the colour of you.

Every other colour in the shell is a shade of grey between paper and ink. The brown is the one hue that isn't, so it should carry something structural rather than an occasional inline quote. Proposal: brown marks everything the user authored, wherever it is re-presented back to them. That makes it frequent — it appears at the top of every history row, on every plan screen, in every clarifying question.

Four of the six classes are deterministic on the client — app names come from the installed list, measures match a number/duration pattern, quoted text is stored verbatim from the prompt, and the three status words are a fixed vocabulary. A small lexer handles those with zero model involvement and zero risk of the model colouring the wrong thing.

Only chg and hedge need the generator to mark them, and both are inside the same post-run summary step that writes the commit message. One tag pair in that prompt, capped at one of each per sentence.

05 · Voice
Minimal is the voice. Whimsy is a word, never a sentence.

The conversational middle register — "It now warns you before the last pour" — is retired. It is the default voice of every assistant product, it pads every sentence with words that carry nothing, and it is slower to read than either neighbour. Minimal wins on speed and holds up under repetition, which matters when you will read hundreds of these.

The whimsical column reads as too much, and the reason is length, not personality: whimsy was buying its charm with extra clauses. The third column keeps minimal structure and spends the personality on one noun or verb. Same word count as minimal, and it still sounds like something rather than nothing.

PROPOSEDMinimal everywhere; one whim-word allowed, and never in failure

Minimal is the structure, always: what happened, what changed, full stop. Within that, a single vivid word is permitted while waiting, on first run, and in a recovery that ended well — the moments where personality costs nothing. In a failure that is staying broken, zero: the sentence gets shorter, not warmer.

06 · Absence
A skeleton is a promise. Only show one you can keep.

Skeletons are right when something is genuinely coming and its shape is already known — the geometry must match the real thing exactly, or the layout jumps and the skeleton was a lie. They are wrong for an empty result and wrong for a state that may never fill.

Right
Loading a grid we have counted
Exact tile geometry, exact count. Breathing opacity, never a diagonal shimmer — shimmer is a web trope and reads as decoration.
Right
Plan rows arriving one at a time
Rows resolve top-down as the model streams. Varying widths, because the real rows vary — a skeleton of identical bars reads as a progress bar.
Wrong
Skeletons standing in for nothing
A user with no apps waiting for six that will never load. Ghosts of hypothetical apps are the same failure with better intentions.
FEEL ITThe tempting middle: the prophetic skeleton

First run could show six greyed tiles already labelled — Pour Timer, Tip Splitter, Dice — as suggestions in the shape of the thing they'd become. It teaches the grid, makes the empty screen feel inhabited, and is arguably honest because tapping one really does make that app. It also risks looking like a broken load. This is not answerable at a desk; build both and live with each for a few days.

08 · Two systems, not one
The shell has a design system. Each app gets a design philosophy.

The six theme presets are cut. They were scaffolding — something to generate against before there was a real system — and they solve a problem nobody has: nothing is improved by the launcher being mint instead of paper. Worse, they put the personality in the shell, where the apps should be carrying it.

The useful half of the idea survives. A generated app should have a coherent look of its own — and that is guidance at build time, not a dropdown at runtime.

The shell — fixed
Paper, ink, three status hues, brown for your words, two faces plus the quoting italic. Not themeable, not configurable, identical everywhere. It is the frame, and frames do not have opinions.
Each app — its own
No contract, no slot count, no approved palette. An app picks colours that suit what it is for. A dice roller and a medication reminder should not look alike, and neither should have been handed the same four variables to fill in.
PROPOSEDThe presets do not come back as a library either. Guidance teaches concepts, not answers.

The tempting rescue for the six presets is to demote them into build-time reference — a curated library the generator picks from. That is worth resisting, and the reason is sharp: those palettes were themselves generated. Feeding a model a list of colour sets a model produced adds no information; it only narrows the range while pretending to be expertise.

Guidance is worth writing when it teaches something the generator would not otherwise weigh — that colours in one app should sit well together, that a palette has to survive being shrunk to a single tile in the grid, that a medical app and a party trick should not read the same. Named hex sets are only worth including where a concept genuinely cannot be pointed at any other way, as an example of a kind rather than a menu to choose from.

Recommendations for the user are a different question and a better one — someone choosing a look for their own app may well want bundles that go together. That is a feature with a person on the other end of it, not a constraint on the generator, and it is not designed yet.

PROPOSEDThe orb holds one to five actions, and the geometry follows the count

Not four buttons — n buttons. Press and hold, then flick: each action owns a 360/n wedge of travel around the orb, so the gesture is a direction rather than a target. One to three keep fixed, memorable forms; four and five divide evenly. Above five, the wheel stops being blind-reachable and becomes a list.
